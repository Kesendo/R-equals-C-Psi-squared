Dephase every site of a Heisenberg graph along an integer axis that need not be a Pauli letter. F138's paragraph "Off the letters" reads the palindrome there with colours: one direction perpendicular to every axis, with every field along it, so that the axes lie in one plane and the fields along its normal. A colour carries the palindrome; this page asks whether the palindrome needs one. It proves why a bond accepts a colour, splits the condition for the far kernel into a part the bonds own and a part the fields own, and shows that the fields' part is settled wherever the bonds admit nothing but the colours. Heisenberg bonds admit nothing more on trees, rings and unicyclic graphs, and on every graph that removing leaves and deleting bonds whose ends share an axis from cycles brings down to one of those, which this page proves; whether they ever do where a site keeps three or more neighbours in the reduced core, at axes no single turn makes letters, is the open question. ## Setup

N sites on a connected graph, H = Σ_{(i,j)} J_ij·σ_i·σ_j + Σ_l h_l·σ_l with every J_ij ≠ 0 and real and every field h_l real, and on every site l one jump n_l·σ_l along a nonzero integer axis n_l at a positive rate (at a rate γ, the same as the unit jump n̂_l·σ_l at rate γ|n_l|²). A **colour** is a direction m perpendicular to every axis; it is **admitted** when every field lies along it. By F158 the palindrome holds exactly when 𝒲 = {W : [H, W] = 0, (n_l·σ_l) W (n_l·σ_l) = −|n_l|²·W for every l} holds an invertible element, and an admitted colour gives one, ⊗(m·σ), which squares to |m|^{2N}. An operator anticommuting with n·σ on a site is there a combination of the w·σ with w ⊥ n, so 𝒲 lies in the span of the products ⊗_l (w_l·σ), w_l ⊥ n_l: the **far span**. The **slot** of site l is the tensor factor at l: in W it is 2-dimensional, n_l^⊥, while the commutators below can leave it in all of ℝ³ or put the identity there. Its condition [H, W] = 0 is the **far equation**. The axes are integers only for the measurements over GF(p); no proof uses it.

## Lemma 1 (the half-turn law)

For a two-site coupling B = Σ_{k,l} W_kl σ^k ⊗ σ^l with any real 3 × 3 matrix W and nonzero real vectors a, b: (a·σ)⊗(b·σ) commutes with B exactly when R_a·W·R_b = W, where R_v = (2vvᵀ − |v|²·1)/|v|² is the half-turn about v (symmetric, so R_v = R_vᵀ). Equivalently, (Wᵀ·a)×b = 0 = (W·b)×a. For a field h·σ on one site, a·σ commutes with it exactly when R_a·h = h, that is when h ∥ a or h = 0.

*Proof.* (a·σ) σ^k (a·σ) = Σ_l (2a_k a_l − |a|² δ_kl) σ^l, so conjugating by a·σ turns the Pauli vector by the half-turn about a, times |a|². Conjugating B by (a·σ)⊗(b·σ), which squares to |a|²|b|², replaces W by R_a W R_b; the product commutes with B exactly when that leaves B unchanged. For unit a, b, R_a W R_b = W reads 2(aᵀWb)·abᵀ = aaᵀW + Wbbᵀ; multiplying by aᵀ on the left gives aᵀW = (aᵀWb)·bᵀ, by b on the right W·b = (aᵀWb)·a, and conversely those two give the identity back. The field is the one-site case. ∎

F138 writes the cross form for diagonal W, where the transpose does not matter. Without it the form needs a symmetric W: for XX + XY under a = b = x it accepts, and the bond does not commute. For Heisenberg bonds, W = J·1, the law asks R_a = R_b: one colour on both ends of every bond, a single colour per component, and every field along it.

## Lemma 2 (the far equation splits)

For any vectors a, b (complex allowed),

  [σ_i·σ_j, (a·σ_i)(b·σ_j)] = 2i·(a×b)·(σ_j − σ_i),  [h·σ_l, w·σ_l] = 2i·(h×w)·σ_l .

So the commutator of a bond with a product in the far span has exactly one identity factor, at i or at j, and the commutator of a field with it has none. Sort [H, W] by the set of identity factors: W in the far span lies in 𝒲 exactly when

- **(B)** for every site i, Σ_{j~i} J_ij·(w_i × w_j) placed at slot j, the other slots as in W, vanishes (the terms with the identity at i), and
- **(F)** Σ_l (h_l × w_l) placed at slot l vanishes (the terms with no identity),

read linearly on W. (B) does not see the fields, and it is the far equation of the field-free H: its solutions are the far kernel of the bonds alone.

*Proof.* The first identity is a direct expansion, (σ^k)(a·σ) = a_k + i(e_k × a)·σ on each site, summed over k; the second is the one-site product rule. Operators with different sets of identity factors are linearly independent, so each set's part of [H, W] vanishes on its own; the bonds fill the sets of one identity, the fields the set of none. ∎

## The reduction

**Theorem.** If on the graph the bond equations (B) admit only the colours, that is their solutions are spanned by the products ⊗(m·σ) over the colours m, then the palindrome holds exactly when an admitted colour exists.

*Proof.* An admitted colour is in 𝒲 and invertible. Conversely, suppose no colour is admitted. If the axes span space there is no colour, (B) has no solution, and 𝒲 = 0. If some field has a component along its own axis, the palindrome fails by F158 §(f5): the odd word built there from H and that one jump has trace 2^N·h_l·n_l ≠ 0. Otherwise every field is perpendicular to its axis, and the colours span either one line m (the axes span a plane) or, when all axes are parallel to one n, the plane S ⊥ n, where the solutions of (B) are the symmetric tensors over S.

- *One line.* W = c·m^⊗N, and (F) reads Σ_l (h_l × m) at slot l, m elsewhere. The part of slot l perpendicular to m comes from the term l alone, so h_l × m = 0 for every l: every field along m, which is then admitted.
- *A plane.* A field h_l ⊥ n lies in S, and h_l × w for w ∈ S points along n; the terms with n at slot l come from the field at l alone, so W contracted at slot l with the in-plane turn n × h_l vanishes. For a symmetric tensor over S, read as a homogeneous polynomial P of degree N in two variables, that is a directional derivative ∂_q P = 0, so P is a power of the one linear form that vanishes on q, and W ∝ ĥ_l^⊗N with ĥ_l the direction of h_l. Two fields of different directions leave W = 0; fields of one direction m make m an admitted colour.

In every case without an admitted colour the palindrome fails. ∎

## Where the bond equations admit only the colours

**Per bond with two different axes.** Put m = n_i × n_j and p_i = n_i × m, p_j = n_j × m, so that {m, p_i} spans n_i^⊥ and {m, p_j} spans n_j^⊥. The output of (B) at i in slot j along n_j comes only from the bond (i, j) (every other term leaves slot j in n_j^⊥), and likewise the output of (B) at j in slot i along n_i. Write the pair's **slot matrix**, W = Σ_{a,b} u_a ⊗ v_b ⊗ M_ab with bases u of n_i^⊥ and v of n_j^⊥ and M_ab tensors over the other slots; it satisfies Σ_{a,b} M_ab·det(u_a, v_b, n_j) = 0 and Σ_{a,b} M_ab·det(u_a, v_b, n_i) = 0. On the pairs (m, m) and (p_i, p_j) both determinants vanish, since p_i, p_j, n_i, n_j all lie in the plane ⊥ m; on the mixed pairs (m, p_j) and (p_i, m) the two determinants form a 2 × 2 system of determinant −|m|⁶ ≠ 0. So **the slot pair lies in span{m⊗m, p_i⊗p_j}**.

**A bond's cross.** For a bond (i, j) and W in the far span, let X_ij(W) be the tensor over the other slots with one vector index obtained by replacing, in each product, the factors at i and j by their cross product w_i × w_j, extended linearly. The term of the bond in (B) at i is J_ij·X_ij(W) placed at slot j, and its term in (B) at j is the same tensor with the opposite sign, placed at slot i. For a solution W and a bond with two different axes, W = m⊗m⊗A + p_i⊗p_j⊗K at the pair by the lemma above, with A and K tensors over the other slots, and since (n_i × m) × (n_j × m) = det(n_i, n_j, m)·m = |m|²·m, the cross is **X_ij(W) = |m|²·m ⊗ K**: only the p_i⊗p_j part reaches the other end, as a multiple of m.

**A bond that drops out.** If X_ij(W) = 0, the bond (i, j) contributes to no equation, and what it leaves is the symmetry of its pair: for every setting of the other slots the slot matrix M, read as a 3 × 3 matrix in the coordinate vectors x̂_a of ℝ³, satisfies Σ_{a,b} M_ab·(x̂_a × x̂_b) = 0, so M = Mᵀ. Two bonds drop out this way for every solution:

- *A bond on one axis.* If n_i ∥ n_j (parallel or antiparallel), w_i × w_j lies along n_j for every pair of slot vectors, so the whole term of the bond in (B) at i points along n_j at slot j, where no other term reaches; hence X_ij(W) = 0. The pair is then a symmetric tensor over the common plane.
- *A leaf.* If i has the single neighbour j, (B) at i is the one term J_ij·X_ij(W), so X_ij(W) = 0. The slot matrix has its rows in n_i^⊥, its columns in n_j^⊥ and is symmetric, so both lie in n_i^⊥ ∩ n_j^⊥: with two different axes M is a multiple of m·mᵀ and **both slots carry m**; with one axis on both, M is a symmetric tensor over the common plane.

So Sol(G), the solutions of (B) on G, are those on the graph without that bond (and without the leaf's slot, which is then free in n_i^⊥) whose pair (i, j) is symmetric.

**Trees.** On every tree (B) admits only the colours. *Proof.* By induction on N, with one site as the base, where (B) is empty and every vector of n^⊥ is a colour. Remove a leaf i with neighbour j; the solutions on G are W ∈ Sol(G − i) ⊗ n_i^⊥ with the pair (i, j) symmetric, and Sol(G − i) is the colours of G − i by induction.

- The axes of G − i span space: Sol(G − i) = 0.
- They span a plane with normal m: W = m^⊗(N−1) ⊗ u, and the slot matrix u·mᵀ is symmetric only for u ∥ m, which needs m ⊥ n_i; then W is a multiple of m^⊗N, a colour, and otherwise W = 0, while the axes of G span space.
- They all lie along n, Sol(G − i) = Sym^{N−1}(n^⊥). If n_i ∥ n, W is symmetric in the slots of G − i and under the transposition of i and j, hence fully symmetric: Sym^N(n^⊥), the colours of G. If n_i ∦ n, the leaf's slot matrix is a multiple of m·mᵀ with m = n_i × n, so slot j lies along m; a symmetric tensor over n^⊥ whose slot j lies along m has every slot along m (contract any slot with a functional vanishing on m and use the symmetry), so W is a multiple of m^⊗N, a colour. ∎

This contains N = 2 and the chain at N = 3, and it settles leaves that share their neighbour's axis. Removing leaves one at a time reduces every connected graph to its **2-core** (what remains when no leaf is left), and the induction step uses only that Sol(G − i) is the colours of G − i, never that G − i is a tree, so it carries the colours from the 2-core back out: (B) admits only the colours on G whenever it does on G's 2-core. A bond on one axis lying on a cycle can be deleted the same way, since the graph stays connected with the same sites and axes, hence the same colours, and the colours are symmetric in every pair. Removing leaves and deleting such bonds, as long as either is possible, ends in the **reduced core**; (B) admits only the colours on G whenever it does there. The reduced core depends on the order of the steps, but whether it is a single site or a single cycle does not: removing a leaf keeps the number of independent cycles, each deletion lowers it by one, and every maximal set of such bonds whose deletion keeps the graph connected has the same size (they are the bases of a cographic matroid restricted to those bonds), so the end always carries the same number of independent cycles.

**Rings of every length.** On the ring of L ≥ 3 sites (B) admits only the colours. *Proof.* If two neighbours share an axis, their bond drops out and leaves a path, a tree. So let no two neighbours share an axis. With indices mod L, the bond (i, i + 1) has m_i = n_i × n_{i+1}, and its per-bond lemma reads with a_i = n_i × m_i at site i and b_{i+1} = n_{i+1} × m_i at site i + 1: the pair lies in span{m_i⊗m_i, a_i⊗b_{i+1}}, with W = m_i⊗m_i⊗A_i + a_i⊗b_{i+1}⊗K_i there and K_i a tensor over the slots other than i and i + 1. Each site thus carries two such vectors, a_i from the bond to its right and b_i from the bond to its left. Then (B) at site i has exactly two terms,

  −J_{i−1}|m_{i−1}|²·(m_{i−1} at slot i − 1) ⊗ K_{i−1} + J_i|m_i|²·(m_i at slot i + 1) ⊗ K_i = 0 .

(a) *One vanishes, all vanish.* If K_i = 0, the first term alone vanishes, so K_{i−1} = 0; around the ring, either every K_i is zero or none is.

(b) *A nonzero K needs a right hinge.* The slots i − 1 and i + 1 are different since L ≥ 3. Contracting slot i − 1 with a functional vanishing on m_{i−1} kills the first term, so the slot i − 1 of K_i lies along m_{i−1}: K_i = m_{i−1} ⊗ K′_i, with K′_i ≠ 0 whenever K_i ≠ 0. Now contract slot i + 1 of W with a functional that vanishes on m_i: what is left is a_i ⊗ K_i, which at the pair (i − 1, i), slots reordered, reads m_{i−1} ⊗ a_i. The per-bond lemma of the bond (i − 1, i) still holds after the contraction, since slot i + 1 lies outside that pair, so m_{i−1} ⊗ a_i must lie in span{m_{i−1}⊗m_{i−1}, a_{i−1}⊗b_i}; its slot i − 1 is m_{i−1}, so the a_{i−1}⊗b_i part vanishes and a_i ∥ m_{i−1}, unless K_i = 0. Since a_i = n_i × m_i is perpendicular to m_i in n_i^⊥, that is m_{i−1} ⊥ m_i: the planes (n_{i−1}, n_i) and (n_i, n_{i+1}) meet at a right angle, the dihedral angle φ = π/2 of the lune in the holonomy table below.

(c) *Every K zero.* Every pair is of the form m_i ⊗ m_i, so slot i lies along m_{i−1} and along m_i. Either all m_i are parallel, to one m perpendicular to every axis, and W is a multiple of m^⊗L, a colour, or W = 0.

(d) *No K zero.* By (b) every hinge is right, so e_i = m̂_i and f_i = m̂_{i−1} form an orthonormal basis of n_i^⊥ at every site. The bond (i, i + 1) admits m_i ⊗ m_i = e_i ⊗ f_{i+1} and a_i ⊗ b_{i+1}, and nothing else; a_i lies along f_i by the right hinge at i, and b_{i+1}, perpendicular to m_i in n_{i+1}^⊥, lies along e_{i+1}, so the second is along f_i ⊗ e_{i+1}: in the letters e and f, neighbours carry different letters. On an odd ring no such word exists, and W = 0. On a four-site ring the case is empty: right hinges at every site make m₁ and m₃ both perpendicular to m₀ and m₂, which forces two neighbours onto one axis. At L = 6 it is not empty, the letters (y, z, x, y, z, x) being one example. On an even ring two words are left, W = a·(e f e f …) + b·(f e f e …), the first with e on the even sites. For odd i the a⊗b part of the bond (i, i + 1) comes from the first word alone and that of the bond (i − 1, i) from the second (for even i the roles swap), so (B) at i reads a·P + b·Q = 0 with two products P and Q, each multiplied by a nonzero factor (J, |m|² and the lengths of a and b, all nonzero). Both carry e at slot i − 1 and f at slot i + 1; at slot i + 2, which is none of i − 1, i, i + 1 since an even ring has L ≥ 4, the two words carry different letters. So P and Q are linearly independent, and a = b = 0. ∎

So **the rule is exact on every connected graph whose reduced core is a single site or a single cycle**: on trees, rings and unicyclic graphs among them, with Heisenberg bonds of any nonzero weights, axes anywhere and fields of any direction, the palindrome holds exactly when the axes have a common perpendicular m with every field along it.

**Axes the letters reach by one turn.** A global turn changes neither Heisenberg bonds nor the dimension of the far end, so where the axes are all parallel, or pairwise parallel or perpendicular, the field-free far end is read at letter axes by [PROOF_PALINDROME_COMPLEMENT_CONNECTION](PROOF_PALINDROME_COMPLEMENT_CONNECTION.md), on any connected graph: with all axes along one n, Theorems 1 and 2 give N + 1 good components, the popcount shells, whose flat sections span the symmetric tensors over the plane ⊥ n; with two letters Theorem 3 gives one, the third letter's colouring, and with three none. Each is the colours' dimension, so (B) admits only the colours there, and the reduction applies. In particular, **with all axes parallel the rule is exact on every connected graph at fields of any direction**: the palindrome holds exactly when every field is perpendicular to the common axis and all of them are parallel to each other. That reaches the fields Theorem 1 leaves out under Heisenberg bonds, a field along X + Y on every site among them; under XX + YY bonds it is not claimed here.

**Beyond.** At a site with three or more neighbours in the reduced core, (B) keeps three or more terms, and step (a) of the rings no longer follows: one K can vanish while the others cancel among themselves. (XX + YY bonds carry sums such as stage E's for another reason: they lie outside Lemma 2's split, and stage E is a chain.) For Heisenberg bonds the GF(p) count says the cancellation does not happen: `TiltedAxesTests.Heisenberg_Bond_Equations_Admit_Exactly_The_Colours` builds (B) in integers on 270 rows (30 each on the chain at N = 2 to 5, the ring at N = 3 to 5, and the star and K₄ at N = 4), with random signed weights and axes generic, coplanar, parallel or in clusters of two and three, and finds the nullity over GF(p), an upper bound, equal on every row to the colours' dimension (N + 1, 1 or 0), an exact lower bound. On the chain, the ring and the star that is a check of the theorems; on K₄, whose sites all have three neighbours, it is exact on each row and, as a statement about every K₄, a measurement and not a proof.

## The same reading as holonomies

Theorem 2 of the complement connection holds for any Hermitian H with Z on every site, so it applies after turning each site's axis to z. Its far end is the flat sections of a connection on the bitstrings, d_y/d_x = H_xy/H_x̄ȳ, and a component carries one exactly when (i) |H_xy| = |H_x̄ȳ| on its edges, (ii) H_xx = H_x̄x̄ on its vertices and (iii) every cycle has trivial holonomy. In the turned frame a basis state is a product of spinors along ±n_l, and σ_i·σ_j = 2·SWAP − 1 makes every entry of a bond a product of two spinor overlaps; flipping every bit sends each spinor to its antipode. The holonomies then read as angles, each the doubled phase of a loop of overlaps. With α_l = s_l·n̂_l and s_l = (−1)^{x_l} at the starting vertex x:

| loop from x | holonomy | reading | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| triangle of pair flips, x → x⊕e₀⊕e₁ → x⊕e₀⊕e₂ → x | ((d + i·det)/(d − i·det))², d = 1 + a·b + b·c + c·a, (a, b, c) = (s₀n̂₀, −s₁n̂₁, −s₂n̂₂) | twice the solid angle of the three axes | derived; exact at four triples and all eight vertices |
| chain, the two pair flips in turn | e^{∓4iφ}, φ the angle at n₁ between the planes (n₀, n₁) and (n₁, n₂), the sign set by the bit of site 1 | the lune between n₁ and −n₁ | floating point, random weights |
| chain, single flips at the sites 2 and 1 in turn | e^{2i·arg(u_⊥·v_⊥ + i·a·(u×v))}, a = α₁, u = J₀₁α₀ − J₁₂α₂, v = J₀₁α₀ + J₁₂α₂ | the triple product det(n₀, n₁, n₂) | derived; floating point, random weights |
| all axes along z, fields at the angles θ₀ (site 0) and θ₂ (site 2) in the plane, 000 → 100 → 010 → 001 → 000 | e^{2i(θ₂ − θ₀)} | twice the angle between the fields | derived; floating point, weights 0.5 and −2 |

The reverse orientation of each loop gives the inverse. The overlaps around the triangle group into two Bargmann loops ⟨a|b⟩⟨b|c⟩⟨c|a⟩, whose phase is half the solid angle (Pancharatnam), and the complement reverses every loop's orientation, which doubles the phase in the ratio; the bond weights cancel in it (checked in floating point at random weights). Along z a field flip from 0 to 1 at site l has the entry h_x − i·h_y and its complement h_x + i·h_y, the ratio e^{−2iθ_l}, while the hops have ratio 1; that is the fourth row. On the triangle the values of d for the four sign classes of the vertices sum to 4, so they cannot all vanish, and with the axes not coplanar (det ≠ 0, no two axes parallel, every pair-flip entry nonzero) some vertex carries a nontrivial loop. At the letters X, Y, Z the solid angle is the octant's π/2 and the holonomy is the −1 of Theorem 3 (d).

The triangle and the single-flip square are trivial at every vertex exactly when the axes are coplanar (at a single vertex the triangle can also be trivial where d = 0, as for the axes (1, 1, 1), (−1, 1, 1), (1, −1, 1) at the vertices 000 and 111); the lune is trivial also at a right angle φ = π/2, where the letters X, Z, Y have no colour, which is why Theorem 3 needs its parity step there and why the rings' step (b) admits a nonzero K only there, before step (d) removes it, and the single-flip square, the triple product, is Theorem 3's parity step off the letters. A single flip at a site is the neighbours' field turning it: its entry is ⟨α_l|m_l·σ|−α_l⟩ with m_l = Σ_{k~l} J_lk·α_k, the complement turns m_l with the spinors, so the ratio is −w/w̄ and condition (i) holds without fields. A field does not turn with the complement: condition (ii) asks it to be perpendicular to its own axis, and then condition (i) asks it to be perpendicular to every neighbour's axis, the local half of the reduction's field step.

## Scope, and what would falsify it

Lemma 1 holds for any real coupling matrix and any nonzero real vectors. Lemma 2 and the reduction hold for Heisenberg bonds of nonzero real weights on a connected graph with every site watched along an integer axis, fields of any direction. The rule is proven on every connected graph whose reduced core is a single site or a single cycle, and on every connected graph with the axes all parallel or pairwise parallel or perpendicular. A counterexample to the measured part would be a Heisenberg row, every site watched, whose bond equations admit a solution that is no colour, or, through the reduction, a palindrome with no admitted colour; `TiltedAxesTests` would report it as a nullity above the colours' dimension. Unwatched sites are outside: there the SWAP rows of F138 (b) pair without a colour at letter axes already. Other bonds are outside Lemma 2's split as stated: their commutators with a product can leave terms without an identity factor.
