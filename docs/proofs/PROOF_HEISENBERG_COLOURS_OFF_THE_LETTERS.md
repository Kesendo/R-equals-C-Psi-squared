Dephase every site of a Heisenberg graph along an integer axis that need not be a Pauli letter. F138's paragraph "Off the letters" reads the palindrome there with colours: one direction perpendicular to every axis, with every field along it, so that the axes lie in one plane and the fields along its normal. A colour carries the palindrome; this page asks whether the palindrome needs one. It proves why a bond accepts a colour, splits the condition for the far kernel into a part the bonds own and a part the fields own, and shows that the fields' part is settled wherever the bonds admit nothing but the colours. Heisenberg bonds admit nothing more, on every connected graph: a bond whose ends share an axis drops out and leaves a symmetric pair, a site where two bonds on different axes meet at an angle neither straight nor right admits nothing, and elsewhere every bond asks both its ends onto its line, the direction n_i × n_j, or neither, which leaves at most two words and lets the site equations keep only the colour. XX + YY bonds split the same way but lack the per-bond lemma below, and they do admit more already on the chain at N = 3, through a sum of strings (F138's turned stage E row).

## Setup

N sites on a connected graph, H = Σ_{(i,j)} J_ij·σ_i·σ_j + Σ_l h_l·σ_l with every J_ij ≠ 0 and real and every field h_l real, and on every site l one jump n_l·σ_l along a nonzero integer axis n_l at a positive rate (at a rate γ, the same as the unit jump n̂_l·σ_l at rate γ|n_l|²). A **colour** is a direction m perpendicular to every axis; it is **admitted** when every field lies along it. By F158 the palindrome holds exactly when 𝒲 = {W : [H, W] = 0, (n_l·σ_l) W (n_l·σ_l) = −|n_l|²·W for every l} holds an invertible element, and an admitted colour gives one, ⊗(m·σ), which squares to |m|^{2N}. An operator anticommuting with n·σ on a site is there a combination of the w·σ with w ⊥ n, so 𝒲 lies in the span of the products ⊗_l (w_l·σ), w_l ⊥ n_l: the **far span**. The **slot** of site l is the tensor factor at l: in W it is 2-dimensional, n_l^⊥, while the commutators below can leave it in all of ℝ³ or put the identity there. Its condition [H, W] = 0 is the **far equation**. The axes are integers only for the measurements over GF(p); no proof uses it.

## Lemma 1 (the half-turn law)

For a two-site coupling B = Σ_{k,l} W_kl σ^k ⊗ σ^l with any real 3 × 3 matrix W and nonzero real vectors a, b: (a·σ)⊗(b·σ) commutes with B exactly when R_a·W·R_b = W, where R_v = (2vvᵀ − |v|²·1)/|v|² is the half-turn about v (symmetric, so R_v = R_vᵀ). Equivalently, (Wᵀ·a)×b = 0 = (W·b)×a. For a field h·σ on one site, a·σ commutes with it exactly when R_a·h = h, that is when h ∥ a or h = 0.

*Proof.* (a·σ) σ^k (a·σ) = Σ_l (2a_k a_l − |a|² δ_kl) σ^l, so conjugating by a·σ turns the Pauli vector by the half-turn about a, times |a|². Conjugating B by (a·σ)⊗(b·σ), which squares to |a|²|b|², replaces W by R_a W R_b; the product commutes with B exactly when that leaves B unchanged. For unit a, b, R_a W R_b = W reads 2(aᵀWb)·abᵀ = aaᵀW + Wbbᵀ; multiplying by aᵀ on the left gives aᵀW = (aᵀWb)·bᵀ, by b on the right W·b = (aᵀWb)·a, and conversely those two give the identity back. The field is the one-site case. ∎

F138 writes the cross form for diagonal W, where the transpose does not matter. Without it the form needs a symmetric W: for XX + XY under a = b = x it accepts, and the bond does not commute. For Heisenberg bonds, W = J·1, the law asks R_a = R_b: one colour on both ends of every bond, a single colour per component, and every field along it.

## Lemma 2 (the far equation splits)

For any vectors a, b (complex allowed),

  [σ_i·σ_j, (a·σ_i)(b·σ_j)] = 2i·(a×b)·(σ_j − σ_i),  [h·σ_l, w·σ_l] = 2i·(h×w)·σ_l .

So the commutator of a bond with a product in the far span has exactly one identity factor, at i or at j, and the commutator of a field with it has none. Sort [H, W] by the set of identity factors: W in the far span lies in 𝒲 exactly when

- **(B)** for every site i, Σ_{j~i} J_ij·(w_i × w_j) placed at slot j, the other slots as in W, vanishes (the terms with the identity at i), and
- **(F)** Σ_l (h_l × w_l) placed at slot l vanishes (the terms with no identity),

read linearly on W. (B) does not see the fields, and it is the far equation of the field-free H: its solutions are the far kernel of the bonds alone.

*Proof.* The first identity is a direct expansion, (σ^k)(a·σ) = a_k + i(e_k × a)·σ on each site, summed over k; the second is the one-site product rule. Operators with different sets of identity factors are linearly independent, so each set's part of [H, W] vanishes on its own; the bonds fill the sets of one identity, the fields the set of none. ∎

## The reduction

**Theorem.** If on the graph the bond equations (B) admit only the colours, that is their solutions are spanned by the products ⊗(m·σ) over the colours m, then the palindrome holds exactly when an admitted colour exists.

*Proof.* An admitted colour is in 𝒲 and invertible. Conversely, suppose no colour is admitted. If the axes span space there is no colour, (B) has no solution, and 𝒲 = 0. If some field has a component along its own axis, the palindrome fails by F158 §(f5): the odd word built there from H and that one jump has trace 2^N·h_l·n_l ≠ 0. Otherwise every field is perpendicular to its axis, and the colours span either one line m (the axes span a plane) or, when all axes are parallel to one n, the plane S ⊥ n, where the solutions of (B) are the symmetric tensors over S.

- *One line.* W = c·m^⊗N, and (F) reads Σ_l (h_l × m) at slot l, m elsewhere. The part of slot l perpendicular to m comes from the term l alone, so h_l × m = 0 for every l: every field along m, which is then admitted.
- *A plane.* A field h_l ⊥ n lies in S, and h_l × w for w ∈ S points along n; the terms with n at slot l come from the field at l alone, so W contracted at slot l with the in-plane turn n × h_l vanishes. For a symmetric tensor over S, read as a homogeneous polynomial P of degree N in two variables, that is a directional derivative ∂_q P = 0, so P is a power of the one linear form that vanishes on q, and W ∝ ĥ_l^⊗N with ĥ_l the direction of h_l, for each nonzero h_l. Two fields of different directions leave W = 0; fields of one direction m make m an admitted colour.

In every case without an admitted colour the palindrome fails. ∎

## Where the bond equations admit only the colours

**Per bond with two different axes.** Put m = n_i × n_j and p_i = n_i × m, p_j = n_j × m, so that {m, p_i} spans n_i^⊥ and {m, p_j} spans n_j^⊥. The output of (B) at i in slot j along n_j comes only from the bond (i, j) (every other term leaves slot j in n_j^⊥), and likewise the output of (B) at j in slot i along n_i. Write the pair's **slot matrix**, W = Σ_{a,b} u_a ⊗ v_b ⊗ M_ab with bases u of n_i^⊥ and v of n_j^⊥ and M_ab tensors over the other slots; it satisfies Σ_{a,b} M_ab·det(u_a, v_b, n_j) = 0 and Σ_{a,b} M_ab·det(u_a, v_b, n_i) = 0. On the pairs (m, m) and (p_i, p_j) both determinants vanish, since p_i, p_j, n_i, n_j all lie in the plane ⊥ m; on the mixed pairs (m, p_j) and (p_i, m) the two determinants form a 2 × 2 system of determinant −|m|⁶ ≠ 0. So **the slot pair lies in span{m⊗m, p_i⊗p_j}**. Nothing here reads the rest of the graph: it holds for every bond with two different axes, whatever else meets its ends, and for every setting of the other slots.

**A bond's cross.** For a bond (i, j) and W in the far span, let X_ij(W) be the tensor over the other slots with one vector index obtained by replacing, in each product, the factors at i and j by their cross product w_i × w_j, extended linearly. The term of the bond in (B) at i is J_ij·X_ij(W) placed at slot j, and its term in (B) at j is the same tensor with the opposite sign, placed at slot i. For a solution W and a bond with two different axes, W = m⊗m⊗A + p_i⊗p_j⊗K at the pair by the lemma above, with A and K tensors over the other slots, and since (n_i × m) × (n_j × m) = det(n_i, n_j, m)·m = |m|²·m, the cross is **X_ij(W) = |m|²·m ⊗ K**: only the p_i⊗p_j part reaches the other end, as a multiple of m.

**A bond on one axis drops out.** If n_i ∥ n_j (parallel or antiparallel), w_i × w_j lies along n_j for every pair of slot vectors, so the whole term of the bond in (B) at i points along n_j at slot j, where no other term reaches; hence X_ij(W) = 0 for every solution. The bond then contributes to no equation, and what it leaves is the symmetry of its pair: for every setting of the other slots the slot matrix M, read as a 3 × 3 matrix in the coordinate vectors x̂_a of ℝ³, satisfies Σ_{a,b} M_ab·(x̂_a × x̂_b) = 0, so M = Mᵀ, a symmetric tensor over the common plane. So Sol(G), the solutions of (B) on G, are those on the graph without that bond whose pair (i, j) is symmetric.

**Theorem (every connected graph).** On every connected graph (B) admits only the colours.

*Proof.* By induction on the number of bonds, with one site as the base, where (B) is empty and every vector of n^⊥ is a colour. The induction is used only by Step 0; on a graph without a bond on one axis, Steps 1 to 3 give the result directly.

*Step 0, a bond on one axis.* Let the bond e = (i, j) have n_i ∥ n_j; by the paragraph above, Sol(G) is Sol(G − e) with the pair (i, j) symmetric.

- If e lies on a cycle, G − e is connected with the same sites and axes, so Sol(G − e) is the colours of G by induction, and the colours are symmetric in every pair (m⊗m, or a symmetric tensor over the common plane).
- If e is a bridge, G − e is two connected graphs G₁ ∋ i and G₂ ∋ j, and Sol(G − e) = Sol(G₁) ⊗ Sol(G₂), since each site's equation acts on its own component's slots. By induction each factor is its component's colours. Write n for the common axis of i and j. A component whose axes span space has no colour, and then W = 0. A component whose axes span a plane has the one colour m₁^⊗, with m₁ ⊥ n since n is among its axes; a component with every axis along n has the symmetric tensors over n^⊥ (a single site among them). Two lines m₁ and m₂: the pair m₁ ⊗ m₂ is symmetric only for m₁ ∥ m₂, a colour of G. A line m₁ and the symmetric tensors: the symmetric pair puts slot j along m₁, and a symmetric tensor over n^⊥ with one slot along m₁ has every slot along m₁ (contract any slot with a functional vanishing on m₁ and use the symmetry), so W is a multiple of m₁^⊗N, a colour of G. Both symmetric tensors: every axis of G is along n, and W is symmetric within G₁, within G₂ and under the transposition of i and j, which together generate every permutation of the slots, so W lies in Sym^N(n^⊥), the colours of G.

So let every bond have two different axes, and N ≥ 2. The direction m_ij = n_i × n_j of a bond lies in n_i^⊥ and in n_j^⊥; its line is the bond's **line** at either end, and by the per-bond lemma the pair (i, j) of every solution lies in span{m⊗m, p_i⊗p_j}, with p_i = n_i × m_ij the other line of n_i^⊥ and p_j that of n_j^⊥.

*Step 1, a site at an oblique angle.* Let two bonds (i, j) and (i, k), j ≠ k, meet at i at an angle φ between their lines with cos φ·sin φ ≠ 0; then W = 0. Write slot i in the basis (m̂_ij, p̂_ij), p̂_ij = n̂_i × m̂_ij, so that m̂_ik = c·m̂_ij + s·p̂_ij and p̂_ik = −s·m̂_ij + c·p̂_ij with c = cos φ, s = sin φ; the slots other than i, j, k ride along linearly. The lemma for (i, k) reads W = m̂_ik ⊗ (m̂_ik at k) ⊗ U + p̂_ik ⊗ (p̂_k at k) ⊗ V, with U and V carrying slot j; write U = m̂_ij ⊗ U₁ + p̂_j ⊗ U₂ there, and V likewise. The lemma for (i, j) asks the component of slot i along m̂_ij to carry m̂_ij at slot j, and the component along p̂_ij to carry p̂_j. The first component is c·(m̂_ik at k) ⊗ U − s·(p̂_k at k) ⊗ V, whose p̂_j part c·(m̂_ik at k) ⊗ U₂ − s·(p̂_k at k) ⊗ V₂ must vanish, so U₂ = V₂ = 0; the second is s·(m̂_ik at k) ⊗ U + c·(p̂_k at k) ⊗ V, whose m̂_ij part gives U₁ = V₁ = 0. So W = 0.

*Step 2, two words.* Otherwise the lines of the bonds at each site are pairwise equal or perpendicular, so they lie among the two lines of an orthonormal frame {e_i, f_i} of n_i^⊥, f_i = n̂_i × e_i (if only one line occurs, take it as e_i). At each end of a bond, its line is one frame line and p is the other. Expand W in the product basis of the frames, W = Σ_w c_w ⊗_l w_l, w_l ∈ {e_l, f_l}, a **word**. The per-bond lemma, holding for every setting of the other slots, makes c_w = 0 for every word that carries the bond's line at exactly one end of some bond. With x_l = 1 where w_l = e_l and 0 otherwise, a surviving word satisfies x_i ⊕ x_j = t_ij on every bond, with t_ij fixed by the frames (1 when the bond's line is e at exactly one of its ends); the frames **agree** around a cycle when the t_ij on it sum to 0 modulo 2. On a connected graph these equations have no solution or two, x and its complement: at most one word w and the word w̄ that changes every letter, and if w survives so does w̄. So W = a·w + b·w̄.

*Step 3, the site equations.* At each bond a word carries the line at both ends, (m, m), or at neither, (p, p), and w̄ does the other. The cross of an (m, m) pair is m × m = 0. That of a (p, p) pair is (n_i × m) × (n_j × m) = |n_i × n_j|²·m by the bond's cross above, nonzero and along the bond's line at slot j, which is the letter w̄_j when the pair is (p, p) under w. Write S for the bonds that are (p, p) under w. Then (B) at site i reads

  a · Σ_{j ~ i, (i,j) ∈ S} κ_ij · [w on V∖{i, j}, w̄_j at j] + b · Σ_{j ~ i, (i,j) ∉ S} κ′_ij · [w̄ on V∖{i, j}, w_j at j] = 0,

each neighbour of i contributing one term and every κ nonzero (J_ij times the length of the cross in the frames). Distinct words are linearly independent. Two terms of one sum differ at the slot of either neighbour. A term of the first sum with neighbour j and one of the second with neighbour j′ differ at j when j = j′; when j ≠ j′ they agree at j and j′ and differ on V∖{i, j, j′}, which is not empty from N = 4 on. So from N = 4 on every coefficient vanishes: a = 0 as soon as some bond lies in S, and b = 0 as soon as some bond lies outside it. At N ≤ 3 (one bond, the three-site chain or the triangle) read the sites one at a time: a site whose bonds lie all in S, or all outside it, sets a or b to zero alone, and a site with one bond in S and one outside gives a·κ + b·κ′ = 0, which sets the other once one is zero; at N = 2 and on the three-site chain each end has a single bond, and on the triangle, whatever S, some site has both its bonds on one side, while S being neither empty nor every bond leaves some site split. Either way, a ≠ 0 needs S empty and b ≠ 0 needs every bond in S, and the two cannot both hold. If a ≠ 0, every bond carries its line at both ends under w, so at each site every line is the one along w_i, two ends of a bond share it, and on a connected graph every line is along one m, perpendicular to every axis: w = m̂^⊗N, a colour. Likewise for b. So W is a multiple of a colour. ∎

So **the rule is exact on every connected graph**: with Heisenberg bonds of any nonzero weights, every site watched along an axis anywhere and fields of any direction, the palindrome holds exactly when the axes have a common perpendicular m with every field along it. With all axes parallel that reads: every field perpendicular to the common axis, and all of them parallel to each other.

**What the steps read.** Step 1 is the right hinge: two bonds at a site leave it free only where the planes (n_j, n_i) and (n_i, n_k) meet straight or at a right angle, the lune row's φ = 0 or π/2 in the holonomy table below. Step 2 is a parity. Around a cycle past Step 1 the frames agree exactly when the cycle turns at an even number of right hinges, so an odd cycle of right hinges carries no word. A triangle on three different axes that passes Step 1 is straight at every site (one straight site makes the axes coplanar, and then every site is straight) or right-hinged at every site, an odd count, so with its axes spanning space it never reaches Step 3; neither does K₄, whose triangles would all have to be coplanar, which puts all four axes in one plane. Graphs whose every cycle turns at an even number of right hinges do: the six-ring right-hinged at every site, and graphs with three-neighbour sites such as the theta graph, the five-ring with a chord and K₃,₃, where the site equations do the last cut. At the letters, Theorem 3 of the complement connection is the same parity read on bitstrings, and its Theorems 1 to 3 give the bond equations' solutions on every connected graph whose axes are pairwise parallel or perpendicular independently of this proof, a second route there.

**The second route.** The GF(p) count builds (B) in integers. `TiltedAxesTests.Heisenberg_Bond_Equations_Admit_Exactly_The_Colours` does so on 270 rows (30 each on the chain at N = 2 to 5, the ring at N = 3 to 5, and the star and K₄ at N = 4), with random signed weights and axes generic, coplanar, parallel or in clusters of two and three, and finds the nullity over GF(p), an upper bound, equal on every row to the colours' dimension (N + 1, 1 or 0), an exact lower bound. `The_Colour_Law_Holds_Where_Its_Last_Step_Has_Content` pins integer rows whose two words reach Step 3 (straight or right at every site, the frames agreeing around every cycle, the axes spanning space): the six-ring, the theta graph, the five-ring with a chord and K₃,₃, nullity 0 on each. The witness at `inspect --root colourlaw` reads the far kernel without the split, from the commutator in the Pauli-string basis with the fields, on fourteen rows.

## The same reading as holonomies

Theorem 2 of the complement connection holds for any Hermitian H with Z on every site, so it applies after turning each site's axis to z. Its far end is the flat sections of a connection on the bitstrings, d_y/d_x = H_xy/H_x̄ȳ, and a component carries one exactly when (i) |H_xy| = |H_x̄ȳ| on its edges, (ii) H_xx = H_x̄x̄ on its vertices and (iii) every cycle has trivial holonomy. In the turned frame a basis state is a product of spinors along ±n_l, and σ_i·σ_j = 2·SWAP − 1 makes every entry of a bond a product of two spinor overlaps; flipping every bit sends each spinor to its antipode. The holonomies then read as angles, each the doubled phase of a loop of overlaps. With α_l = s_l·n̂_l and s_l = (−1)^{x_l} at the starting vertex x:

| loop from x | holonomy | reading | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| triangle of pair flips, x → x⊕e₀⊕e₁ → x⊕e₀⊕e₂ → x | ((d + i·det)/(d − i·det))², d = 1 + a·b + b·c + c·a, (a, b, c) = (s₀n̂₀, −s₁n̂₁, −s₂n̂₂) | twice the solid angle of the three axes | derived; exact at four triples and all eight vertices |

The reverse orientation of each loop gives the inverse. The overlaps around the triangle group into two Bargmann loops ⟨a|b⟩⟨b|c⟩⟨c|a⟩, whose phase is half the solid angle (Pancharatnam), and the complement reverses every loop's orientation, which doubles the phase in the ratio; the bond weights cancel in it (checked in floating point at random weights). Along z a field flip from 0 to 1 at site l has the entry h_x − i·h_y and its complement h_x + i·h_y, the ratio e^{−2iθ_l}, while the hops have ratio 1; that is the fourth row. On the triangle the values of d for the four sign classes of the vertices sum to 4, so they cannot all vanish, and with the axes not coplanar (det ≠ 0, no two axes parallel, every pair-flip entry nonzero) some vertex carries a nontrivial loop. At the letters X, Y, Z the solid angle is the octant's π/2 and the holonomy is the −1 of Theorem 3 (d).

The triangle and the single-flip square are trivial at every vertex exactly when the axes are coplanar (at a single vertex the triangle can also be trivial where d = 0, as for the axes (1, 1, 1), (−1, 1, 1), (1, −1, 1) at the vertices 000 and 111); the lune is trivial also at a right angle φ = π/2, where the letters X, Z, Y have no colour, so there the pair-flip loops give no obstruction and the rest falls to the parity and the site steps (at the letters Theorem 3's steps (c) to (e), here Steps 2 and 3, after Step 1 has left the site free only there); the single-flip square at two bonded sites, the triple product, is one such obstruction read as an angle. A single flip at a site is the neighbours' field turning it: its entry is ⟨α_l|m_l·σ|−α_l⟩ with m_l = Σ_{k~l} J_lk·α_k, the complement turns m_l with the spinors, so the ratio is −w/w̄ and condition (i) holds without fields. A field does not turn with the complement: condition (ii) asks it to be perpendicular to its own axis, and then condition (i) asks it to be perpendicular to every neighbour's axis, the local half of the reduction's field step.

## Scope, and what would falsify it

Lemma 1 holds for any real coupling matrix and any nonzero real vectors. Lemma 2 and the reduction hold for Heisenberg bonds of nonzero real weights on a connected graph with every site watched along an integer axis, fields of any direction. The rule is proven on every connected graph. A counterexample would be a Heisenberg row on a connected graph, every site watched, whose bond equations admit a solution that is no colour, or, through the reduction, a palindrome with no admitted colour; `TiltedAxesTests` would report it as a nullity above the colours' dimension, the witness as a disagreement. A disconnected graph is outside: there the bond equations' solutions are the product of the components' own, and without fields so is the far kernel (two bonds apart under generic axes keep one element where the law, written for one component, would say none), while fields couple the components through (F) (two isolated sites with equal fields along their common axis keep σ⁺⊗σ⁻ and σ⁻⊗σ⁺). Unwatched sites are outside: with two of them the SWAP rows of F138 (b) pair without a colour at letter axes already; with one, at letter axes and letter fields, F165's Theorem 5 proves the colour rule, and off the letters this page says nothing about it. Other bonds still split by identity factors, since for any two-site bond Σ W_kl σ^k⊗σ^l the commutator with (a·σ_i)(b·σ_j) is 2i[((Wᵀa)×b)·σ_j + ((W·b)×a)·σ_i], one identity factor in every term; what they lack is the per-bond lemma, whose cross w_i × w_j is the case W ∝ 1, and XX + YY bonds pair without a colour on the chain at N = 3.
