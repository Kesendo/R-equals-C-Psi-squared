# Sodium in Agarose, Layer by Layer

**Date:** 2026-10-27
**Authors:** Thomas Wicht, Claude (Opus 5.5)
**Status:** A reading (Tier 4) of published measurements, drawn as stacked filters. The script recomputes every number on the page and checks the readings from the tables by asserts; the water, diffusion and line-width values are read off the paper's figures and only printed, and the integrals of its Figs. S5 and S16 are transcribed from the numbers printed in them; the band widths are our choice, and what depends on them says so. No new physics: the model is the standard two-step picture of quadrupolar relaxation, a fast motion plus a slow one (Halle and Wennerström, *J. Chem. Phys.*, 1981; recalled, not checked here).
**Script:** [`sodium_knowledge_layers.py`](../simulations/sodium_knowledge_layers.py)

[The Nucleus as a Key](THE_NUCLEUS_AS_A_KEY.md) reads the three spectral densities J₀, J₁, J₂ of a sodium nucleus back to the motion around it, and names one check: under a single Lorentzian density, J₀/J₂ − 1 = 4·(J₀/J₁ − 1). Its open item was to hold that check against measured rates. This page does, with one published data set, and draws what it finds the way the gallery draws the jumps of a nucleus ([The Sodium Nucleus, Layer by Layer](../visualizations/README.md#the-sodium-nucleus-layer-by-layer)): every piece of knowledge is a transparent layer, a band of the plane that agrees with it, and light passes only where every layer agrees.

## The data

Nimerovsky, Sieme and Rezaei-Ghaleh measured ²³Na in agarose gels of 3 to 15 % at 105.84 MHz, combining single-quantum relaxation with triple-quantum-filtered experiments, and found three sodium populations: one in slow motion with bi-exponential relaxation, 29 to 45 % of the sodium (their Table S5), and two with mono-exponential relaxation (*Methods* 228 (2024) 55, open access under CC BY). For the slow population their Supplementary Table S4 gives J₀, J₁ and J₂ for six samples, in the convention T₂ₛ = 1/(J₀ + J₁), T₂ₗ = 1/(J₁ + J₂), T₁ₛ = 1/(2J₁), T₁ₗ = 1/(2J₂); the table's header says Hz, and the values work as rates in s⁻¹ (1/(J₀ + J₁) gives the tabulated T₂ₛ). They report no error bars on these values, and for the 9 % sample one of the two triple-quantum experiments was not run.

Their own analysis meets the key's check directly. Their main text describes the successful fit as a slightly modified single Lorentzian with one additional flexible parameter (their equation S3), and their equation S4, 0.25·(J₀/J₂ − 1) = α·(J₀/J₁ − 1), is the single-motion relation with a factor α that one Lorentzian fixes at 1; run on the Table S4 values it gives α = 0.32 ± 0.02 (R² = 0.94). They do not quote a correlation time, giving as the reason the lack of accurate data on the quadrupolar coupling constant of sodium in the gels.

The values show why the relation fails: J₁/J₂ lies between 1.13 and 1.34, almost flat between ω₀ and 2ω₀, while J₀/J₁ lies between 13 and 21. One Lorentzian cannot do both: if J₀/J₁ sets (ω₀τ)², near 12 to 20, then J₁/J₂ would have to be near 4; if J₁/J₂ sets it, it is near 0.05 to 0.13. These J₁ and J₂ are the least robust numbers of the paper. A first analysis that tied J₂ to one Lorentzian (their Table S3) gave J₁/J₂ near 3.7 to 3.8 with nearly the same J₀, and the authors note the two analyses differ notably in J₁ and J₂; Table S4 is the one that fits all their data and conditions, and the reading below rests on it.

## The layers

A density that is flat at high frequency and large at zero is what two motions give: a fast one, in extreme narrowing and so equal at 0, ω₀ and 2ω₀, plus a slow one. Write it as

  J_m = a + b / (1 + m²·(ω₀τ)²),   m = 0, 1, 2,

with a the flat part and b = J₀ − a, so a ≤ J₂. Every point of the plane (ω₀τ, a) then predicts J₁ and J₂, and each thing known about a sample becomes a layer:

- **J₁ and J₂**, the measured values, each a band of ±10 % relative (our choice, since the source gives none);
- **τ = 12 ns**, a rotational correlation time for sodium in 2.4 % agarose (Lundberg and Kuchel, *Magn. Reson. Med.* 37 (1997) 44, as cited by the authors; a lower concentration than here, from a triple-quantum study by its title, its analysis not checked here), a band of ±2 ns (our choice);
- **free ion**: the flat part at least that of free Na⁺ in the same apparatus, 1/(2·56 ms) ≈ 8.9 s⁻¹, from the reference solution in the same supplement. This is a one-sided bound and an assumption: the gel slows the water, as the authors' ¹H and ¹⁷O data show, and the fast motion is taken to keep the free ion's coupling.

## What the picture shows

**The bottom edge is dark.** At a = 0 the model is one Lorentzian, and there the J₁ band and the J₂ band stand apart in every agarose sample, near ω₀τ ≈ 3.5 to 4.4 and ≈ 1.9 to 2.6. That is the authors' α = 0.32 seen as a gap.

**Higher up, the measured densities allow a stretch, and it starts near 9 ns.** With a flat part the two bands bend together. Within ±10 % they overlap over a stretch of ω₀τ rather than at a point, because J₁ − J₂ is only a few s⁻¹ and a tenth of each covers most of it. What J₀, J₁ and J₂ allow without the two outside layers (J₀ fixes b; the product of the J₁ and J₂ bands above one half, printed by the script):

| slow part b (s⁻¹) | 172 | 143 | 258 | 345 | 433 | 497 |

So in the two-motion model the densities rule out a slow motion faster than about 9 ns in every sample at ±10 % bands, and the upper end is loose. The lower end moves with the band width we chose: about 10 ns at ±5 %, about 7 to 8 ns at ±20 %, where the upper end leaves the plot. The independent 12 ns falls inside every stretch, and the white spot is where it crosses: its height comes from J₁ and J₂, its horizontal place mainly from the τ layer. The exact reading without bands, between 11.4 and 13.1 ns, is a point with no spread attached, and the authors' modified Lorentzian, read from J₀/J₁ alone (J₀/J₁ − 1 = α·(ω₀τ)², our computation, not theirs), gives 9 to 12 ns for the same values. Both readings land near the independent number, so the 12 ns does not tell the two models apart; it is compatible with both.

**The free-ion layer is a bound, not a test.** It covers the upper part of every agarose panel. Read at the exact reading, the flat part sits just above it at 3 % (9.9 and 10.9 against 8.9 s⁻¹) and moves away from it as the gel gets denser (18.2 at 15 %). The flat part mixes the speed of the fast motion and the strength of its coupling, so the picture alone cannot say which of the two changes.

**The paper's other views, laid beside the flat part.** Two more measurements of the same samples can be laid beside the flat part, not drawn in the figure. The water: the authors' ¹⁷O T₁ of water falls from 6.86 ms in the reference to about 3.1 ms at 15 % (their Fig. 1E, values below read off the figure by eye, one 3 % point for both 3 % samples), and in extreme narrowing 1/T₁ follows the water's rotation times its quadrupolar coupling. The mobile sodium: the authors fit one common T₁ to the two mono-exponential populations, being unable to tell them apart (their Table S6). A mono-exponential T₁ means J₁ = J₂, so 1/(2T₁) is the mobile sodium's density at ω₀ and 2ω₀. It is not shown to be a flat density: in the authors' fit the larger mobile population has T₂ ≪ T₁ (their Table S5), which they call unexplained and which a paragraph below finds outside quadrupolar relaxation if the fit's amplitudes are taken as they stand, a premise that turns on how the echoes were read.

The flat part grows roughly with the water's ¹⁷O rate, below it in every sample, and in five of six samples it is between 0.82 and 0.84 of the mobile sodium's density at ω₀ in the same gel, at 12 % 0.75, the sample that also departs in the next section. In the two-step picture the ratio is (χ²_bound/χ²_mobile)·(1 − S²)·(τ_fast,bound/τ_fast,mobile), with S² the share the slow motion carries, provided the mobile density at ω₀ is wholly fast. Only with equal couplings and equal fast correlation times would it read as 1 − S² ≈ 0.83, S² ≈ 0.17; and the mobile density at ω₀ is the fit's, whose large mobile population a paragraph below finds hard to reconcile with quadrupolar relaxation, unless the fit's amplitudes are inflated by its readout. What stands without those assumptions is the observation: one ratio of two high-frequency densities, nearly constant while the gel concentration fivefolds. Further cautions: the ¹⁷O values are read off a figure; the authors note that the ¹⁷O changes exceed the ¹H ones, which may mean the water's coupling changes too, the same ambiguity as in a; and Table S6 comes from the authors' joint fit of all three populations, so this layer is not fully independent of Table S4.

**Three views, to tell the coupling from the motion.** In extreme narrowing a single rate sees only a product, coupling times motion, the regime where [the key page](THE_NUCLEUS_AS_A_KEY.md#back-to-the-motion) notes that strength and motion can no longer be told apart. A second view helps only if it is blind to one of the two. For the water the paper has three views of the same gels, and for the sodium two, each blind somewhere else:

- **diffusion** D (their PFG data, Figs. 1A and 2D) sees no coupling; but over the tens of milliseconds of the measurement a molecule travels micrometres, far beyond the pores, so D is slowed mainly by the detour around the fibres, which rotation does not feel;
- **¹H T₁** of water (Fig. 1C) sees rotation times a dipolar coupling largely fixed inside the molecule, plus an intermolecular part, and in agarose also proton exchange with the hydroxyl groups and cross-relaxation to the polymer (agarose is a standard magnetization-transfer phantom), so it is not a pure rotation clock;
- **¹⁷O T₁** of water sees rotation times a quadrupolar coupling set by the electric field gradient and its asymmetry, which can change; the sodium's flat part a, in the two-step picture, sees the coupling times the fast share 1 − S² times the fast correlation time.

The water's views are read off the figures by eye (the reference at 0 % first, one 3 % point for both 3 % samples; a pixel re-measurement by a reviewer agreed within 1.5 %), each given as a ratio against 0 %; the row for a is computed from Table S4 through the two-motion reading and taken against the free-ion bound of 8.9 s⁻¹:

This is how we saw it, as a first reading of these data, and it is kept as seen. The ¹H view ran nearly with diffusion (¹H ÷ D within about ten percent of 1), which looked like the water's change being mostly motion; the ¹⁷O view grew beyond it, to 1.43 at 15 %, which looked like the coupling, the field gradient at the oxygen growing with the gel, as the authors suspected from ¹⁷O outrunning ¹H. The sodium's flat part showed the same pattern: a outruns the sodium's diffusion, a ÷ D rising to 1.45, close to the water's ¹⁷O ÷ D at 9 % and 15 %. Read together: the gel slows the motion and, on top, strengthens the field gradient that water and sodium feel, perhaps the same gain for both, the sodium carrying its water shell.

What the lenses said against it, and it is a lot. Diffusion is mostly the gel's geometry, so a rotational rate divided by D mixes the coupling with the obstruction, and ¹H ÷ D ≈ 1 may be two different mechanisms meeting by chance. The ¹H view carries exchange and magnetization transfer. The sodium's D is most likely that of the mobile populations, the slow population's short T₂ hiding it from the echo, so a ÷ D sets one population against another. A growing a can be a smaller order parameter as well as a larger coupling. The scatter between the two 3 % samples, which share one D point, is about ±5 % in a ÷ D, and within it four of six samples agree with the water's ¹⁷O ÷ D; only 12 % departs, the same sample that departs in the table before, where the sodium's D also stops falling between 12 and 15 %. The cleaner comparison is ¹⁷O against ¹H, both rotational, which rises to 1.29 at 15 %; that may be a growing coupling, or reorientation at the fibre surface that is slower about one axis than another, which the two nuclei see differently. Whether the reading is right we do not know.

**What the rate table says about the population the authors could not explain.** The authors' fit carries a mobile population, about half of the sodium, with a mono-exponential T₂ far below T₁ (their Tables S5 and S6), and they call the mechanism unclear, pointing to anisotropic motion. The rate table of [the sodium page](THE_SODIUM_NUCLEUS_TWO_MIRRORS.md) gives a statement that does not depend on how the sodium is split into populations. In this page's convention the central-transition coherence decays at J₁ + J₂, the T₂ₗ rate, and the longitudinal components at 2J₁ and 2J₂, so the central rate is exactly the mean of the two longitudinal rates (the sodium page writes the same rates three times larger, 3(J₁ + J₂), 6J₁, 6J₂). This holds at every J₀, every residual splitting and every offset: the jumps cannot move weight into or out of the central coherence, and any static term diagonal in m only shifts its frequency. The script checks it exactly on the repo's sodium generator. Two consequences:

- A single fitted longitudinal rate is about 0.4·J₁ + 1.6·J₂, so the central transition decays at most 2.5 times faster than that rate, the extreme reached only as J₂ → 0, where T₁ turns strongly bi-exponential.
- The central transition carries 40 % of the transverse signal after a hard pulse. The inversion recovery is essentially mono-exponential in every gel (their Fig. S4, side peaks of 0.01 to 0.04 at most; the slow population is bi-exponential in T₂, while its two T₁ parts nearly coincide since J₁/J₂ is only 1.13 to 1.34), so at least 40 % of each population's transverse signal should decay with a T₂ near T₁, no shorter than T₁/2.5.

The authors' fit does otherwise. It splits the sodium into a slow population p₃b, which relaxes bi-exponentially with the J₀, J₁, J₂ of Table S4, and two mobile populations relaxing mono-exponentially, a large one p₁ and a small one p₂ (their Table S5, in percent, p₁ + p₂ + p₃b = 100). Its share of transverse amplitude decaying near T₁ is 0.4 of the slow population (its T₂ₗ) plus the small mobile population (its T₂ near T₁), about a quarter; the large mobile population gets T₂ six to twelve times below its T₁:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| common T₁ of the mobile populations (ms), Table S6 | 38.6 | 42.2 | 35.2 | 30.2 | 26.2 | 22.8 |
| T₂ of the large mobile population (ms), Table S5 | 5.45 | 6.9 | 3.55 | 2.9 | 2.55 | 1.85 |
| T₁ ÷ T₂ | 7.1 | 6.1 | 9.9 | 10.4 | 10.3 | 12.3 |

So within secular Redfield relaxation by rank-2 quadrupolar fluctuations, for any J₀, J₁, J₂ and any static term diagonal in m, this population cannot be what the fit makes it: a spectral density can give 60 % of a population a T₂ of a few milliseconds (the satellites, at J₀ + J₁), never a mono-exponential T₂ that far below T₁. Anisotropic motion, which the authors point to, is covered as long as it acts through J₀, J₁, J₂ and a residual splitting. Exchange between sites without a difference in their frequencies keeps every |m⟩⟨m′| where it is and so keeps the central coherence decoupled, at a rate between the sites' own values of J₁ + J₂; with such a difference it adds to the decay, which is one of the candidates below (argued here, not computed: the script checks one site, and the sodium page's model has no exchange). The small mobile population is off the model too, in the other direction: a mono-exponential T₂ needs J₀ = J₂, and with J₁ = J₂ that is T₂ = T₁, while the fit has T₁ ÷ T₂ = 1.2 to 1.4 for it. The extra populations came from the fit's design as much as from the data: the authors found that the slow population alone did not reproduce the echo curves (their Fig. S11), nor, for the one sample they tried, with one mono-exponential population added (Fig. S12), and wrote that the additional populations should have T₂ values similar to T₂ₛ or T₂ₗ, since their distributions show mainly two exponentials; the large one indeed sits next to the slow population's T₂ₛ of Table S4 (5.09, 6.08, 3.51, 2.64, 2.14, 1.85 ms), and its T₁ is the common T₁ of both mobile populations, which the inversion recovery could not tell apart. So the leading reading is that the three-population split absorbs something.

**What the fit's readout weighs.** The authors' simulation of the echo data records the signal over an acquisition time t_acq after each mixing time t_mix (the delay of the echo below) and Fourier transforms it, S(t_mix) = FFT{S(t_mix; t_acq)} (their Section 2.2.4.3); its Hamiltonian, as described there, holds no offset and no inhomogeneous width. Which number of that spectrum enters the fit is not said. If it is the point at the line's centre, that point is the time integral of the recorded signal, and each component enters with its amplitude times its own T₂ (our inference, not the authors' statement). Weighted that way, the fit's fast components (0.6·p₃b + p₁) carry a fifth to a third of the simulated signal, and that is what the authors' own inverse Laplace transform of their simulated curves shows at the shortest filter (their Fig. S16, the integrals printed beside the peaks). Their transform of the measured curves (Fig. S5) gives the measured signal's fast part a similar share; that is the datum, not a second confirmation of the weighting, since any good fit makes the two transforms agree:

(At 9 % two measured peaks lie below 7 ms, 0.17 and 0.08; at 6 % a further 0.07 sits near 15 ms and is not counted.) So the fit's three quarters of fast amplitude sit behind a signal whose fast part is a fifth to a third. Where the factor T₂ enters, the figures do not fix, and three readings give the same printed numbers:

- the simulation reads the centre point, and the measured echoes were read as an area or as the signal's first point (the text does not say; for the sodium diffusion data it names the peak height). Then the fit's amplitudes are inflated by the readout, the measured signal shares are the true amplitudes, and about two thirds or more of the sodium decays near T₁: nothing conflicts with the 40 % floor, and the large mobile population would be largely the price of fitting an area with a peak, a fast part of a fifth to a third remaining;
- the simulation reads the centre point and the measured echoes were read the same way. Then the fit's amplitudes stand, up to the bias of the width the simulation lacks (the static-spread bullet below), and so does the conflict;
- the factor T₂ comes from how the transform's peaks are integrated (a T₂ distribution on logarithmic bins, integrated over T₂, weighs each bin by its width, which grows with T₂), and the simulation reads an area. Then too the fit's amplitudes stand, and the conflict with them.

One route does not depend on the echoes' readout: the 1D spectrum after a single pulse, whose line shape carries the true amplitudes. Its width (their Fig. 2B, read off by eye, a pixel re-measurement agreeing within 0.1 Hz, one 3 % point for both 3 % samples) against the line the amplitudes of each reading predict, each component a Lorentzian of width 1/(πT₂) widened by the instrument's own part. That part is matched to the 0 % reference, 7.3 Hz measured against 1/(π·56.3 ms) from its T₂ (Table S7): 1.6 Hz if it is a Lorentzian, 3.4 Hz if it is a Gaussian, or taken as none. A static spread can only widen the measured line, so a negative entry is a reading the line does not allow. With the fit's amplitudes the line comes out narrower than predicted in every sample if the instrument's part is a Lorentzian, and at 3 % for every non-zero instrumental part (the 0 % reference shows that part is not zero); with a Gaussian part or none, 6 to 15 % pass. The first reading leaves room everywhere, room that a static spread of a few hertz would take. That leans toward the first reading and does not decide it: the verdict turns on the instrument's shape, which is not known, the 3 % width is one point read off a figure for two samples, and the prediction is sensitive to the narrow components' T₂. The rest of this section asks what follows if the conflict is real (the second and third readings). Which reading holds, we do not know.

**What the echo leaves.** The T₂ values come from a double spin echo: a 90° pulse, a delay Δ, a 180° pulse, Δ again, then t_mix/2, a second 180° pulse and t_mix/2, after which the signal is recorded (their Fig. S3C). If the conflict is real, the echo sorts what could cause it, and the reason is where things sit. Label each level by two bits as [the sodium page](THE_SODIUM_NUCLEUS_TWO_MIRRORS.md) does, bit a whether it is outer (|m| = 3/2) or inner (|m| = 1/2), bit b the sign of m (the bits, not the flat and slow parts a and b of the layers above). Then:

- the first-order quadrupolar splitting, a multiple of T₂₀ = (3I_z² − 15/4)/√6, is a Z on bit a alone (the sodium page's own reading);
- an offset, I_z = Z_b + ½·Z_a Z_b, needs bit b;
- the central transition |½⟩⟨−½| keeps bit a (both levels inner) and flips bit b, the satellites |±3/2⟩⟨±½| flip bit a;
- an ideal hard 180° pulse, which maps m → −m, flips bit b and leaves bit a alone.

So the pulse and the central transition live on bit b, the splitting on bit a, and they never meet. That is one fact read twice: the central transition does not see the splitting, and the pulse does not refocus it. An offset does meet the pulse: flipping b reverses the sign of Z_b and of Z_a Z_b, so the phase the coherence gathers before the pulse is gathered with the opposite sign after it and cancels at the echo. That a Hahn echo refocuses shifts and not a first-order quadrupolar splitting is textbook NMR; what the bits add is the reason in one line, and one more thing: no pulse that acts on each bit separately (a site-local monomial sandwich, the class the repo's Theodolite reads) reverses an offset and a splitting together, since reversing Z_b needs a flip of b, reversing Z_a Z_b then forbids a flip of a, and Z_a keeps its sign. (In Souza et al.'s labelling, |3/2⟩ … |−3/2⟩ as |00⟩ … |11⟩, the same splitting is Z⊗Z and the central transition |01⟩⟨10|; [The Sodium Chain](THE_SODIUM_CHAIN.md) uses that one, with the central part of I_x and I_y commuting with Z⊗Z, and the central transition being free of J₀, the slow fluctuation of the same operator, is Souza et al.'s statement quoted on the sodium page.)

The script reads this with the repo's own instruments and checks it on the relaxation generator (the Lindblad superoperator of the sodium page): the letter cube finds that Z_a moves no Pauli string of the central transition and every string of the satellites; the Theodolite's letter layer finds the flip of b reversing the offset and keeping the splitting, a flip of a reversing the splitting, and no plain sandwich reversing both (its transposed branch returns ρ ↦ ρᵀ, which reverses every real diagonal Hamiltonian and is no pulse); and on the generator, exactly, the central coherence is coupled to no other element, with the entry −3(J₁ + J₂) − iω in the sodium page's units (J₁ + J₂ in this page's) and no ω_Q in it. Two candidates are narrowed by it:

- **A residual splitting**, its secular first-order part, static or fluctuating at any speed, does not touch the central 40 %. A distribution of splittings can dephase the satellites inside the echo, which no 180° pulse refocuses, and so raise the share decaying fast to 60 %, not to the fit's three quarters (0.6·p₃b + p₁, 74 to 76 %). So it cannot be the whole of what the split absorbs; it can be a part. This holds for pulses that are hard against the splitting (their 90° pulses of 23.6 to 24.0 µs are a nutation frequency of 1/(4·24 µs) ≈ 10 kHz, so splittings well below that; the pulses are not ideal, since the first point of every inversion recovery deviated from −1 and the authors adjusted the inversion angle in their simulation), the 3 : 4 : 3 weights after the first pulse, and the fit's amplitudes taken as they stand, the second or third reading above. The authors set this candidate aside for a different reason, that it would spoil their joint fit of the two triple-quantum experiments.
- **A static spread of frequencies**, a T₂* from the gel's susceptibility, a shift or the field, is odd in m, and the echo refocuses it during the echo delays. It does not refocus it during the recording that follows, and there it changes only the weights, the same at every t_mix: it lowers the peak of a narrow component more than that of a broad one, so a peak-height fit whose simulation has no such width shifts amplitude toward the fast components, by an amount that depends on the width. With imperfect pulses part of the unrefocused signal can also pass: both 180° pulses carry the same phase φ₂ (Fig. S3C), so the phase cycle does not tell the pathways across them apart, and only the pulses' quality does; pathways stored along z add signal that relaxes at T₁, pathways that stay transverse without being refocused add signal that decays with the static spread. Under the fit's amplitudes the 1D line width leaves the spread between no room and a few hertz, depending on the instrument's shape (the first row of the table above), so it neither shows nor excludes one.

What is left, if the conflict is real:

- the readout itself: a width during the recording that the simulation lacks, which biases a peak-height fit toward the fast components;
- exchange or diffusion between places of different central frequency, a chemical, susceptibility or second-order quadrupolar shift that moves with the ion, at any exchange rate: two sites with populations p and 1 − p, frequencies δ apart and exchanging at rate k still add p(1 − p)·δ²/k in the fast limit (the two-site exchange of dynamic NMR; the sodium page borrows its Bloch-McConnell form as an analogy for the J₂ jumps and keeps chemical exchange outside its model);
- fluctuating fields of rank 1, a paramagnetic trace or the dipolar coupling to protons, whose J(0) acts on the central transition (a fluctuating offset, Z_b and Z_a Z_b, the letters that do move it) and which the mean-rate identity, a statement about rank-2 quadrupolar relaxation, does not cover;
- slow motion of the full quadrupolar coupling beyond the Redfield picture: its second-order shift of the central transition is odd in m, so the echo refocuses it only where it holds still, and its motion during the echo survives;
- the fit's design, with T₂ values chosen near T₂ₛ and T₂ₗ.

How large each of these could be here is not estimated on this page. Which, we do not know.

**The tissue panels are a contrast, read with care.** Their numbers come from Table 1 of the review *²³Na relaxometry: an overview of theory and applications* (PMC12406536): fast and slow T₂ and a single T₁, which the script reads as the weighted mean 0.4·J₁ + 1.6·J₂ of the bi-exponential T₁, an approximation. The values were taken from an automated extraction of that table and not checked against the original. Cartilage at 3 T gives a white spot from its own densities alone, which with only these two layers is the crossing that is always there when the model can be solved, and no test. Calf muscle at 1.5 T gives J₂ ≈ −1.4 s⁻¹ against J₁ ≈ 83 s⁻¹, so J₂ is near zero within that approximation, and the script lets nothing pass where J₂ ≤ 0. The dark panel says the reading fails for these numbers, not why.

## What this is, and what it is not

Not new: the central transition decaying at J₁ + J₂ and the longitudinal components at 2J₁ and 2J₂ (Hubbard's rates, recalled), the two-step model, reading J(0), J(ω₀), J(2ω₀) against one Lorentzian, the failure of one Lorentzian for these data (the authors' α), the 12 ns, which is a published measurement, that a Hahn echo refocuses shifts but not a first-order quadrupolar splitting, to which the central transition is blind, and that an isolated spin 3/2 under quadrupolar Redfield relaxation cannot have a mono-exponential T₂ far below T₁, which follows from Hubbard's rates and the 3 : 4 : 3 weights; also that a spectral point at the line's centre weighs a component by its T₂, and the fast-exchange broadening p(1 − p)·δ²/k.

What the page adds is the reading: the key's single-motion check found in a published analysis as the same equation, the gap it leaves drawn as two bands that do not meet on the one-motion edge, and, in the two-motion model, a lower end near 9 ns on the slow motion from the densities alone in all six samples at the chosen ±10 % bands, with the independent 12 ns inside every allowed stretch; and beside the paper's other measurements, a flat part that follows the water and, in five of six samples, stays a near-constant fraction of the mobile sodium's density at ω₀ in the authors' fit; and three views of the same gels, diffusion, ¹H and ¹⁷O, laid side by side as a first reading of how the coupling's share might be told from the motion's, kept with the objections it met; and for the authors' unexplained population, the share comparison (a quarter of the fit's amplitude decaying near T₁ against a floor of 40 %), then the readout behind it: weighted by T₂, as a spectral point at the line's centre weighs them (our inference from the paper's definition), the fit's fast components carry a fifth to a third of the simulated signal, matching the authors' transform of their simulated curves, so whether the conflict is in the data or only in the fit turns on where that factor enters and how the measured echoes were read, which the paper does not say; the 1D line width, which needs no echo readout, leans toward no conflict (in every sample for a Lorentzian instrumental part, at 3 % for any) and does not decide; and for the readings where the conflict is real, from where the echo's pulse and the splitting sit on the two bits, read with the repo's letter instruments, that the secular first-order part of a residual splitting cannot be all of it (for hard pulses), that a static spread of frequencies is refocused during the echo though not during the recording, that the line width without an echo decides nothing about such a spread, and that no pulse acting on each bit separately as a diagonal or anti-diagonal factor reverses an offset and a splitting together. Which reading holds, and what the split absorbs in the second, we do not know. It rests on the two-motion model and on the authors' second set of J₁ and J₂, which carry no error bars. A single motion of another shape, or residual order in the gel, where the densities need not follow one curve, could pass the same layers.

## Open

- Error bars: with uncertainties on J₀, J₁ and J₂ the bands get their true width, and the stretch either narrows or spreads.
- A second agarose data set, or a temperature series, where τ should move and the flat part with it.
- The five genuine checks of the key need the individual coherence rates, which the published J values do not give back independently.
- The three views with the authors' numbers instead of figure read-offs, D corrected for obstruction, and a rotational view of the sodium that does not feel the fibres, to tell the coupling's share from the gel's geometry.
- The readout: which number of the spectrum the simulation reads, how the measured sodium echoes were read (peak height, integral or first point) and how the transforms' peaks are integrated decide whether the unexplained population is in the data or only in the fit; the authors can say, a refit of the measured curves under each readout would show it, and the authors' own value of the 3 % line width (and which 3 % sample it is) would sharpen the 1D route.
- A direct look at a residual splitting: a double-quantum filter with a magic-angle pulse, or a nutation experiment, instead of the indirect argument from the triple-quantum fit; and a central-transition-selective echo, which measures the central coherence's decay against the mean of the two longitudinal rates directly.
- What the split absorbs, in the peak-height branch: the sizes of the candidates for these samples, and an echo train with varied refocusing interval (CPMG) would show a spacing dependence for exchange or diffusion between shifts on the time scale of the spacing, and would be blind to fast exchange and to rank-1 fields with short correlation times; the instrument's line shape would turn the line-width comparison into a test.
