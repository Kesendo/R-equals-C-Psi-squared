# One Sodium Pool or More: the Mean-Rate Identity against Published Agarose Data

**Date:** 2026-11-14
**Authors:** Thomas Wicht, Claude (Opus 5.5)
**Status:** A test (Tier 4 reading) of published fitted values against an exact relation of the sodium model. The script recomputes every number on this page except two estimates made in the text, the diffusion lengths and the polarization; the data are read by hand from two open-access papers, some values off their figures; the error bars are the authors' fit uncertainties, taken as independent, and carry none of their systematic errors.
**Script:** [`sodium_one_pool_or_more.py`](../simulations/sodium_one_pool_or_more.py), output in [`output.txt`](../simulations/results/sodium_one_pool_or_more/output.txt) (about seven minutes; `--global` adds a differential-evolution check of the free-and-bound fit)

[The sodium page](THE_SODIUM_NUCLEUS_TWO_MIRRORS.md#what-a-sodium-measurement-can-read) holds one relation between three rates a measurement can read: in a single sodium pool the slow transverse rate is the mean of the two longitudinal rates, whatever the spectral densities, a residual splitting, an offset or a dynamic frequency shift. Its open list asks for that relation against measured rates and for what a voxel with two compartments does to it. This page takes the first published data we found that report all three rates for the same agarose concentrations: 2, 4 and 6 % agarose with 154 mM NaCl at 9.4 T, measured by Reichert, Schepkin, Kleimaier, Zöllner and Schad (2024).

The published fitted values do not fit one pool, and the conflict sits in one place. The measured slow transverse time equals the authors' weighted longitudinal time T₁m within the errors, as one pool with nearly equal J₁ and J₂ would give. Their T₁ fit, with its amplitudes fixed to the one-pool values, reports instead a split of the two longitudinal rates that one pool could only combine with a slow transverse time 14 to 17 % shorter. The mechanisms inside one pool that this page and the agarose page name move the gap the other way, and a signal stored along z by imperfect pulses has the right sign but not the size. Free sodium beside bound sodium, with the free sodium like saline and the fit errors taken as independent, does not reproduce the numbers. The authors' own triple-quantum amplitudes, compared across the three samples, rise in a way that disfavors one pool with the fitted split and that two bound pools can give. What remains is two readings: the fitted split is not one pool's, or there are two pools on scales beyond 10 to 17 µm. The two sequences, simulated pulse by pulse, put the longitudinal triple-quantum signal at a tenth to about a third of what one pool with the fitted split gives, and both readings allow that; for pools that share the acquisition's d no size could tell them apart at first order in time, since one pool has as many unknowns there as the data have numbers and such pools read there as one pool at their mean. Every refit on this page samples the recovery as the first paper's text describes it, where its Fig. 2A shows a longer and sparser tail (the Open list). We do not know which holds; the shape of that signal in time would tell, and it needs the measured curves.

## The relation

Hubbard's rates for one spin 3/2 under secular Redfield relaxation by fluctuating electric field gradients give the longitudinal magnetization two components, with rates 2J₁ (weight 0.2) and 2J₂ (weight 0.8), and the transverse magnetization two, with rates J₀ + J₁ (weight 0.6) and J₁ + J₂ (weight 0.4), where J₀, J₁, J₂ are the spectral densities at 0, ω₀ and 2ω₀ in the convention of the agarose page; the sodium page and the script write the same rates three times larger (6J₁, 6J₂, 3(J₀ + J₁), 3(J₁ + J₂)). Hence

  1/T₂s = (1/T₁s + 1/T₁f)/2.

Here s and f mean slow and fast, as in the two papers; the agarose page uses the subscript s for short, so its T₁ₛ and T₂ₛ are the fast components. The relation is linear in the densities and holds for any shape of the spectral density. When J₁ ≥ J₂, as one spectral-density curve of any distribution of correlation times gives, the component of weight 0.8 is the slower one, the T₁s of a fit that fixes the weights (the first paper's text beside its Eq. 7 gives the slow time as 1/(2J₁), the reverse, while its Eq. 9, a₀ = J₁/J₂ = T₁s/T₁f, has it as here); an ordered medium need not follow one curve ([the sodium page](THE_SODIUM_NUCLEUS_TWO_MIRRORS.md#beyond-spin-32)). The sodium page shows that the rates do not depend on a residual splitting; [the agarose page](SODIUM_IN_AGAROSE_LAYER_BY_LAYER.md) gives the reason for every static term diagonal in m: the jumps cannot move weight into or out of the central coherence, and such a term at most shifts its frequency (an offset and a dynamic shift do; a first-order splitting does not, since it moves the levels m = ±½ alike). The finite polarization at 9.4 T near room temperature (ħω₀/kT ≈ 2·10⁻⁵) and the terms the secular approximation drops are far below anything read here. The authors' own Eqs. 7 and 8 contain the relation; they do not test it.

Two consequences are used below. A single pool with J₁ ≥ J₂ has T₁m/2.5 ≤ T₂s ≤ T₁m, where T₁m, the authors' Eq. 17, is the mean of the two longitudinal rates weighted 0.8 and 0.2; the lower end is the agarose page's floor, the upper end follows the same way. And the relation's gap splits exactly as

  (1/T₁s + 1/T₁f)/2 − 1/T₂s = (1/T₁m − 1/T₂s) + 0.3·(1/T₁f − 1/T₁s),

a part that compares T₂s with T₁m and a part that is the split of the two longitudinal rates. T₁m is the combination the data fix best: in the first paper's Fig. 5 it stays flat while T₁s and T₁f drift apart as the late points are added (from about 47.5 and 35.8 ms to 49.2 and 30.9 ms), so the split comes from the late tail.

## The data

The first paper (NMR in Biomedicine) gives T₁s and T₁f from an inversion recovery with a triple-quantum filter, in its Table 2 for four phase cycles, fitted with its Eq. 13 jointly on the single- and the triple-quantum signal with the amplitudes held at their one-pool values 0.8 and 0.2. Its free fit (its Table 1), joint with the triple quantum as well, was unstable: at 2 and 4 % it put about 93 to 94 % of the single-quantum signal at one time, near 43 and 41 ms, and the other time at the fit's bound, while at 6 % it resolved a split (39 % at 45 ms, 60 % at 29 ms). Its triple-quantum peak (its Fig. 2B) is what the authors take as proof that T₁ is bi-exponential, and their Eq. 16 gives the fitted ratio of its amplitude to the single-quantum amplitude: 5.3, 8.7 and 15.9 % in phase cycle PC2, 3.1, 6.0 and 11.5 % in PC1. Its tabulated T₁m differs from Eq. 17 of its tabulated times by 0.1 to 0.4 ms. Its sampling is described as dense to 65 ms, which its stated step and count would end at 32 ms, and then in steps of 0.8 ms; its Fig. 2A ends the dense band near 65 ms and steps the coarse points by about 1.8 ms to about 640 ms (Part 9). The script samples to 65 ms densely and then in steps of 0.8 ms to 305 ms, as the text's step gives; the Open list carries the difference.

The second paper (Magnetic Resonance in Medicine) gives T₂s, T₂f and their amplitudes, in its Table 1, from a triple-quantum sequence with a refocusing pulse at 26 evolution times between 0.4 and 200 ms, "determined in the same way" as its Eq. 11, which we read as the single-quantum signal alone fitted as a biexponential plus an offset; its 0 % sample gives T₂ = 54.19 ± 0.95 ms. Both papers are at 9.4 T with the same concentrations; neither says that the samples or the temperature were the same. The first paper's Fig. 6A shows its own T₂s values, about 1 ms shorter than the second's (44.1, 38.1 and 32.2 ms, read off the figure), and its Fig. 6C the transverse triple-quantum ratio, 23.1, 27.0 and 33.8 % (read off).

The T₁ values are those of PC2. With the T₁s and T₁f errors fully correlated (the papers give no covariance) the gaps stand at 5.4, 7.8 and 6.7 σ; over the four phase cycles they run from 4.0 to 5.4 s⁻¹; with the first paper's own T₂s values from its figure they are 3.7, 4.6 and 4.0 s⁻¹.

The split above shows where the gap sits. The part comparing T₂s with T₁m is +0.5, +0.5 and −0.4 s⁻¹, and with the authors' tabulated T₁m and both errors T₂s − T₁m = +0.9 ± 1.5, +0.5 ± 1.1 and −0.9 ± 0.8 ms, within the errors. The whole gap is the second part, 3.6, 4.9 and 5.2 s⁻¹, from a split of the two longitudinal rates of 12 to 17 s⁻¹. So the measured T₂s sits where one pool with a nearly mono-exponential T₁ would put it, while the fitted T₁ split asks for a T₂s 14 to 17 % shorter. One of the two does not belong to one pool.

## What can carry the conflict

The mechanisms that can add to the relaxation of one pool, as far as this page and the agarose page name them, move the gap the other way, toward a central coherence that decays faster than the mean of the longitudinal rates:

- **Rank-1 fields**, paramagnetic or dipolar to protons: to first order their parts at ω₀ add equally to the central rate and to the mean of the longitudinal rates, and their part at zero frequency adds to the central rate alone. Their cross-correlation with the quadrupolar part, which the sodium page names, is not argued here.
- **Exchange between sites of different central frequency**: in the fast limit it adds p(1 − p)·δ²/k to the central coherence alone, as the agarose page notes.
- **Slow motion beyond the Redfield picture**: the part of the coupling along T₂₀ commutes with the populations and with the central transition however slowly it moves, and the parts sampled at ω₀ and 2ω₀ stay perturbative while ω_Q ≪ ω₀; what is left is the agarose page's fluctuating second-order shift of the central transition, extra decay.
- **Unrefocused field inhomogeneity and diffusion** shorten T₂ as well.

A distribution of correlation times that every nucleus samples keeps the relation. The authors find that correlation times and coupling strengths computed from the T₁ pair and from the T₂ pair disagree, and read that as "a distribution of correlation times and quadrupole strength parameters". Their comparison mixes this relation with [the key's single-motion check](THE_NUCLEUS_AS_A_KEY.md#back-to-the-motion); a distribution breaks the second and cannot break the first. Over the measurement a sodium ion travels micrometres, far beyond the pores, as the agarose page puts it: in a T₁ of 40 ms about √(6Dt) ≈ 17 µm, or √(2Dt) ≈ 10 µm out of a slab (D ≈ 1.2·10⁻⁹ m²/s, recalled). So each ion samples the gel's microstructure, and the distribution is of the kind that keeps the relation. Three readings remain:

- **(i) The fitted split is not one pool's.** The amplitudes are fixed at their one-pool values, the split grows with the late points, and at 2 and 4 % the free fit found the single-quantum recovery close to mono-exponential, which is what T₂s ≈ T₁m asks for.
- **(ii) More than one pool** of nuclei that do not average during the relaxation, which needs regions beyond 10 to 17 µm with different relaxation, a heterogeneity of the gel on that scale.
- **(iii) The measurement.** With imperfect pulses a signal stored along z relaxes at longitudinal rates and pulls an apparent T₂s toward T₁, the sign seen here ([the agarose page](SODIUM_IN_AGAROSE_LAYER_BY_LAYER.md) names such pathways for its echo). The size does not fit. Added to one pool's transverse signal and fitted with Eq. 11, it would have to be 0.64, 0.56 and 0.36 of that signal if it relaxed at T₁s, 1.6 to 1.7 times it at T₁m, and would lift the slow amplitude to 0.55 to 0.76 against the measured 0.40 to 0.41. Stored along z for half the evolution, even the whole slow signal would decay at 42.9, 37.3 and 32.6 ms, 1.5, 2.6 and 1.0 σ short of the measured T₂s. The sequences simulated pulse by pulse (below) give the part a common flip-angle error carries: at most 0.23, 0.34 and 0.48 of the gap with every flip angle 10 % off.

## Free and bound sodium

The simplest picture of more than one pool is free sodium relaxing like saline, beside one bound pool. The script builds it from the spin-3/2 operators with a pool label beside each nucleus: the free pool in extreme narrowing at T₁ = T₂ = 54.19 ms, the bound pool with J₀ ≥ J₁ ≥ J₂ ≥ 0, exchange with detailed balance. It reads the signals the sequences record, the rank-1 and rank-3 parts after an inversion and after an excitation, and puts them through the authors' fits: Eq. 13 of the first paper for T₁, with the sign between the two longitudinal terms that the physics requires (the printed equation shows a minus) and the triple-quantum part weighted at the reported ratio, and Eq. 11 of the second paper for T₂, on 26 evenly spaced evolution times (their spacing is not stated; geometric spacing is shown beside). Six checks hold: the jump operators, with a Hamiltonian, against the repository's gated sodium generator ([`sodium_two_mirrors.py`](../simulations/sodium_two_mirrors.py)) to 2·10⁻¹⁵, and the voxel's pool block against it to the same; the voxel's signals without exchange against Hubbard's for each pool to 9·10⁻¹⁶; the central-transition cells under exchange, exactly decoupled from the rest, against the closed two-site exchange matrix, to the last digit; a single pool, which comes back from the two fits unchanged; and the fit of Eq. 13 started from swapped times, which still finds them.

- **One pool alone** gives χ² = 26.5, 69.6 and 62.3 for the five independent numbers.
- **Free and bound, without exchange** (four parameters, one degree of freedom): χ² = 15.8, 39.5 and 11.7; with geometric spacing 16.5, 46.0 and 20.2, which makes 2 % the weakest instead of 6 %; halving or doubling the triple-quantum weight changes them by less than one. The free fraction comes out at 4 to 7 %, the model's T₂s stays 3.8, 4.8 and 1.7 σ short and its slow amplitude 1.1, 3.7 and 2.9 σ high. The verdict rests on the free pool's time: at 58 ms the χ² values fall to 12.9, 30.0 and 6.9, at 65 ms to 7.4, 16.5 and 2.4, while the agarose page's mobile populations relax faster than saline, not slower; with the free time fitted under its measured 54.19 ± 0.95 ms they are 15.7, 38.0 and 11.2. Much of the χ² at 4 and 6 % is the slow amplitude, which the paper gives to 0.01 and which pulses and readout move most, and correlations within one fit, which the papers do not give, can move these values substantially either way.
- **With exchange**, profiled over rates from 1 to 1000 s⁻¹: χ² rises with the rate toward the one-pool values, since fast exchange makes one averaged pool.

Under the fit errors taken as independent, and with free sodium like the 0 % sample, the free-and-bound picture fails in all three samples.

## Two bound pools

Two bound pools without exchange, each with J₀ ≥ J₁ ≥ J₂, have seven parameters for five numbers, so the fit does not determine them. What it does constrain is the longitudinal triple-quantum signal, written TQ₁ here (B1 in the script's output) and measured against what one pool with the same fitted times gives (TQ₁ = 1 for one pool). A spread of longitudinal rates across nuclei that are each close to mono-exponential makes little rank-3 longitudinal order. | 4 % | 0.0 at TQ₁ 0.07 | 0.0 | 4.1 | 24.7 |

The lowest χ² at 2 % comes with both pools mono-exponential and no longitudinal triple quantum at all, which the measured peak excludes if that peak is the pools' own rank-3 order. Within χ² of about 2, two pools allow TQ₁ up to about 0.23 at 2 %, 0.37 at 4 % and 0.54 at 6 %: at most between a quarter and a half of one pool's. The transverse triple quantum of these solutions stays at 0.99 to 1.00 of one pool's, in line with the second paper's agreement between its measured and its theoretical transverse signal. A typical solution at 2 %: 91 % of the sodium with longitudinal times 48.5 and 44.7 ms, 9 % with 28.0 and 16.1 ms. The fit with its amplitudes fixed to 0.8 and 0.2 reads such a pair as T₁s and T₁f of one pool; each pool obeys the relation, and the gap is the reading. The table gives values found by a local search; nudged starts move them by a few units of χ², and the band edges are soft.

## The sequences, pulse by pulse

The script's Part 8 runs both sequences on one spin 3/2: hard pulses, the relaxation between them, the eight phase steps and the acquisition. The first paper's Fig. 1 gives the inversion-recovery sequence: the inversion pulse, the evolution time, a 90° pulse whose phase advances by 45° with each step of the evolution time, and after a mixing time the paper does not give (zero in the script) a second 90° pulse at phase 0 before the acquisition; PC1 turns the inversion pulse by 180° in every other scan, PC2 shifts the first 90° pulse by ±135° in two scans. The transverse sequence starts with a 90° pulse, refocuses halfway through the evolution and ends with the same two 90° pulses; the papers do not give its phases. A phase stepped with the evolution time acts as an offset, which turns the orders ±1 at the stepping frequency and the orders ±3 at three times it, as [the sodium page](THE_SODIUM_NUCLEUS_TWO_MIRRORS.md#the-irreducible-tensors-in-strings) has it for the string class that carries both the central transition and the triple quantum; the script splits the signal over the phase steps that way. It reads each acquisition at the line, the integral of its signal, the way the second paper builds its transverse signal (the caption of its Supporting Fig. S2); the first paper does not say.

For one pool with ideal pulses, counting what starts from the first pulse of each sequence (a phase cycle that alternates that pulse with the receiver keeps only that), the two triple-quantum amplitudes, each over its own single-quantum amplitude as the first paper's Eqs. 15 and 16 define them, are

  longitudinal (4/5)·(√5/4)·(√15/8) / ((1/√2)·(1/2)) · |d| = (√6/4)·|d|,  transverse (√6/5)·(√15/8)² / (1/2)² · |d| = (3√6/16)·|d|,

where d is what the acquisition makes of a rank-3 coherence against a rank-1 one, read at the line d = (√6/5)·(T₂f − T₂s)/(0.6·T₂f + 0.4·T₂s) with the times the acquisition decays with, the inverse of the correction factor of the second paper's Eq. 8. Their ratio is 4/3, whatever d is. Eq. 16 divides the longitudinal amplitude by A₁s + A₁f = 1, the recovered single quantum of Eq. 13, and Eq. 15 the transverse one by the single quantum at the start of its decay, as the count does. Both triple-quantum signals pass through the same two coherences |±3/2⟩⟨∓3/2|, the cells |00⟩⟨11| and |11⟩⟨00| in the sodium page's two-qubit names, and leave them through the same last pulse and the same acquisition; they differ in where they start. The longitudinal one starts from the inverted populations, whose rank-3 order is 4/5 times the difference of the two longitudinal exponentials, and is turned into the triple quantum with the Wigner coefficient √5/4; the transverse one starts from the rank-3 coherence, √6/5 times the difference of the two transverse exponentials, and is turned with √15/8. The triple quantum comes back with √15/8 in both, and each single quantum has its own rank-1 coefficients. The two relaxation factors are twice √(4/5·1/5) and √(3/5·2/5), square roots of products of Hubbard's weights; the sodium page states the second as its triple-quantum path, and SodiumTwoMirrorsWitness gates it and both turns of the tensor bases live. Two routes give the amplitudes and meet to 8·10⁻¹⁴, at most four times the rounding that the scans and the evolution allow: the coefficients, which sympy gives exactly, and the sequences simulated, over three choices of the transverse phases (twice the refocusing pulse's phase less the first pulse's: 90°, 30° and −60°). PC1 leaves the longitudinal ratio as it is for an ideal inversion pulse, PC2 for any flip angle: adding the two scans at ±135° multiplies each order p by cos(135°·p), 1/√2 in size for the single and the triple quantum and zero for the double, which the simulation shows order by order to 10⁻¹⁵ with every flip angle 10 % off. The first paper's statement that the transverse pathway gives the larger triple-quantum signal is about the signal's height, the coefficient times the peak of its shape, which is larger there because the two transverse times lie further apart.

The times the acquisition decays with are not known. It is not refocused: the homogeneous T₂ gives the largest |d|, 0.75, 0.89 and 0.94, and any broadening or a finite acquisition window lowers it. The second paper corrects its transverse signal with T₂* (its Table 1); put into the formula, those give 0.57, 0.72 and 0.80, but its two components do not share one broadening at 2 and 6 %, and one Lorentzian broadening shared by both and matched to either gives 0.32 to 0.46 at 2 % and 0.71 to 0.78 at 6 %. [The agarose page](SODIUM_IN_AGAROSE_LAYER_BY_LAYER.md) describes the same unrefocused spread for its own echo, where it moves the weights a peak fit reads. So |d| is at most 0.75, 0.89 and 0.94 and in these models comes down to 0.32, 0.72 and 0.71; everything absolute below carries that range and assumes ideal pulses. With the alternation one pool's ratio stays 4/3 at every choice of the transverse phases for ideal pulses, and over the three pools and the phase choices it lies between 1.26 and 1.35 with every flip angle 5 % off and between 1.18 and 1.42 with 10 %, read as a fit with a free phase reads it; PC1 then reads 1 to 8 % above PC0 and PC2. Without the alternation the magnetization recovering during the evolution is turned by the TPPI-phased 90° pulse into the single and the triple quantum, in quadrature with what starts from the first pulse: at 60 ms it is 0.58 to 1.28 of the transverse triple quantum and 2.7 to 6.4 times the transverse single quantum there, a pathway stored along z like those the agarose page names. A single quantum ruled at late times by a component in quadrature does not look like the published fits, two transverse components with a slow fraction of 0.40 to 0.41, and the numbers here are for the alternated sequence. Fitted like the second paper's Eq. 11 on its 26 evolution times, that sequence's single quantum gives one pool a T₂s longer by up to 0.64, 0.88 and 0.83 ms with every flip angle 5 % off and up to 1.65, 2.30 and 2.15 ms with 10 %, at most 0.23, 0.34 and 0.48 of the gap: the sign of reading (iii), not its size.

The measured ratios then place the longitudinal triple quantum at TQ₁ = 0.10, 0.17 and 0.25 of one pool's with the fitted times in PC1, and at 0.17, 0.24 and 0.35 in PC2 (errors 0.02 to 0.05 from the first paper's Table 2; the transverse values read off its Fig. 6C carry none). That reading assumes the two acquisitions lose alike and does not depend on d; it scales with 4/3 over one pool's actual ratio, by 0.94 to 1.13 over the flip angles 10 % off. Read from the size alone, over the range of d and for ideal pulses, the values are 0.07 to 0.16, 0.11 to 0.14 and 0.20 to 0.26 (PC1) and 0.12 to 0.27, 0.16 to 0.20 and 0.28 to 0.37 (PC2). One pool with the fitted split, TQ₁ = 1, is excluded by the size itself, not only by the trend across the samples: the largest TQ₁ would need one pool's ratio 2.84 times smaller than 4/3, where the flip angles, the phases and the pools above keep it between 1.18 and 1.42, and the two phase cycles disagree by at most 1.7.

Both remaining readings reach these values. Two bound pools held at the measured TQ₁, fitted like everything above to the five numbers of PC2, reach χ² of at most 0.72 in every sample and at either cycle's value; with seven parameters for these six conditions that shows they can, not that they do (the search of [Two bound pools](#two-bound-pools) went from below; this one holds TQ₁ at the value). One pool whose true split is 1.1, 2.2 and 3.5 s⁻¹ (PC1, with its own times and T₁m) or 1.9, 3.4 and 5.4 s⁻¹ (PC2) gives them as well, its triple quantum read through the shape of the published times, as a fit with those times reads it. That pool predicts a T₂s shorter than the second paper's by 0.95, 1.15 and 0.16 σ (PC1; χ² = 2.25 for three, p = 0.52) or by 1.29, 1.95 and 1.24 σ (PC2; χ² = 7.03, p = 0.07), and within 1.1 ms of the first paper's own T₂s (its Fig. 6A, read off without error bars); with one pool's ratio anywhere in 1.18 to 1.42, χ² runs from 2.06 to 2.71 (PC1) and from 6.22 (p = 0.10) to 8.98 (p = 0.03) (PC2). The first paper's Fig. 5 shows how a pool like that can sit behind the fit: as the late points are added, the fitted ratio falls from about 8.6 to 5.3 % while the split widens, and the coefficient times the peak of its own shape, the height of the triple quantum against the single quantum, stays at 0.892 and 0.905 %. The height is the stable datum, and TQ₁ follows the split. So the size does not decide between readings (i) and (ii); under PC2 it leans weakly against (i), under PC1 not at all. The shape in time would: one pool with such a small split puts the peak of its triple quantum near 43.1, 37.2 and 32.2 ms, the published times put it at 38.6, 33.2 and 29.2 ms (PC2), and two pools give a sum of two shapes. That needs the measured curves. For two pools the comparison holds only roughly in one respect: each pool's triple quantum reaches the receiver with its own d, which the search, reading the signal before the acquisition, does not carry.

Counting says why the size cannot decide, at least at first order in time. There one pool has as many unknowns as the data have numbers. The two initial rates are 1/T₁m = 0.4·J₁ + 1.6·J₂ for the recovery and 0.6/T₂f + 0.4/T₂s = 0.6·J₀ + J₁ + 0.4·J₂ for the transverse decay; each triple quantum starts with a slope, its amplitude times its split, (√6/2)·|d|·(J₁ − J₂) for the longitudinal one and (3√6/16)·|d|·(J₀ − J₂) for the transverse one. For densities that fall from J₀ to J₂ these four numbers fix J₀, J₁, J₂ and d exactly once, unless both slopes vanish, as they do where all three densities are equal: 1.2 times the first slope and 3.2 times the second add to √6·|d| times the difference of the two initial rates, which gives d, and the rest follows. So at first order the data measure d, and they have no equality to test one pool with. Their inequalities cannot tell one pool from several either, as long as the pools share d: the four numbers are linear in the densities, so such pools give exactly the first-order numbers of one pool at their mean densities, each pool weighted by its share of the signal read at the line, and each inequality bounds a convex set, which the mean stays in; exchange between the pools keeps each coherence summed over them and drops out of the first order as well. Pools whose acquisitions decay differently do not share d, and then the inequalities can flag a mixture. The published values meet them: |d| comes out at 0.51, 0.59 and 0.74 (0.51, 0.60 and 0.74 in PC2), below the largest values of 0.75, 0.89 and 0.94 and at 4 % also below the broadening models' 0.72, as the transverse amplitude there lies below one pool's; and the densities lie in the region every sum of Lorentzian densities fills, J₀ ≥ J₁ ≥ J₂ ≥ J₀·J₁/(4J₀ − 3J₁), of which the positive slopes already give J₁ ≥ J₂ and J₀ ≥ J₂, so that J₀ ≥ J₁, the lower edge and the largest |d| are the tests. That region's lower edge is one Lorentzian, the single-motion relation of [the key page](THE_NUCLEUS_AS_A_KEY.md#back-to-the-motion), and the published values lie above it by 0.04 to 0.08 in J₂/J₀, with J₁/J₂ between 1.06 and 1.21 and J₀/J₁ between 8 and 16: off one Lorentzian in the direction a sum of them takes, the shape the agarose page finds in its own data. The split of that pool, 2(J₁ − J₂), is 1.28, 2.49 and 3.92 s⁻¹ (PC1) or 2.04, 3.85 and 6.05 s⁻¹ (PC2), 10 to 15 % above the splits of reading (i), which reads the triple quantum through the published shape where this reads its slope; the slope is the less stable of the two, moving by 8.6 % in Fig. 5 where the height moves by 1.5 %. The count is the ideal sequence's: it takes Hubbard's weights 0.6 and 0.4 for the transverse rate, without the rank-3 pathway and its transverse twin of the section below, which shift the weights it takes, and it takes the slopes of the fitted curves and the transverse amplitude of the first paper's figure with the second paper's times. A dynamic frequency shift adds no unknown: the transverse sequence refocuses at half the evolution, where the shift, odd in m, cancels at first order, and in the acquisition it is part of d, which both triple quanta share. The mean-rate identity needs the second order, the split of each single quantum on its own, and the T₂s comparison above is that order.

The simulation shows three more things.

- **A pathway Eq. 13 leaves out.** The first 90° pulse also turns the rank-3 population order into rank-3 coherence of order ±1, which shows at the single-quantum frequency and reaches the receiver during the acquisition. The first paper's discussion expects pathways besides the main one to add to the single quantum and judges them much smaller than it, too small to influence the single-quantum peak. This one is that small: over the range of d it moves 0.020 to 0.058 of the weight from the fast to the slow component, as 0.8 + (√6/40)·|d| gives. With the weights fixed it is not small for the split: the fit reads one pool with the published split as a split 3 to 6 % smaller and a T₁m 0.5 to 1.1 ms longer when its triple-quantum part carries one pool's own weight, and 6 to 17 % smaller and 0.5 to 1.4 ms longer at the reported weight. That moves the gap further from one pool, not toward it; for reading (i), at the largest |d|, it raises the deviations above to 1.02, 1.31 and 0.35 σ (PC1; χ² = 2.86) and to 1.40, 2.21 and 1.69 σ (PC2; χ² = 9.66, p = 0.02).
- **Its transverse twin.** The transverse sequence carries the same kind of term: the rank-3 coherence built during the evolution reaches order ±1 at the second 90° pulse and the receiver through d, and for ideal pulses moves the slow weight of the transverse single quantum to 0.4 + (√6/80)·|d|. The ratio of the two triple quanta does not see it, since the weights still add to 1, but the slow fraction the earlier sections compare with the measured 0.40 to 0.41 does: for one pool it becomes 0.41 to 0.43, and its deviation from the measured value turns from −2.0, −0.6 and −0.8 σ to −0.5 to +1.4, +2.6 to +3.4 and +2.4 to +3.4 σ over the range of d. Flip angles 5 % off move it by as much again (0.413 to 0.444), so the size of that deviation is open; its direction at 4 and 6 %, worse for one pool and for free and bound sodium, whose slow amplitude was already high, survives them. The pool fits of the earlier sections do not carry it.
- **The amplitudes themselves.** Over the range of d one pool's transverse amplitude read at the line is 14.9 to 34.6, 33.1 to 40.9 and 32.5 to 43.3 %, and the measured 23.1, 27.0 and 33.8 % are 0.67 to 1.55, 0.66 to 0.82 and 0.78 to 1.04 of it. The 4 % value rests on the figure: the second paper's transverse maximum (its Table 2), turned into an amplitude with one pool's shape at its T₂, gives 23.3, 31.0 and 35.0 %, the same at 2 and 6 % and 15 % higher at 4 %, where it lies at 0.76 to 0.94 of one pool's. The phase cycles are not understood: the measured PC2 values are 1.72, 1.45 and 1.38 times the PC1 values, while in the model PC2 equals PC0 for any flip angle and a common flip-angle error raises PC1, by 1 to 8 %. The authors read it as the single quantum of PC2 adding imperfectly. One candidate outside exact pulse phases is an error of 5 to 10° in PC2's ±135° step, which multiplies order p by cos(p·(135° + error)): it raises PC2's ratio by 1.35 to 1.68 and leaves 0.17 to 0.34 of PC0's double quantum, which the first paper's Fig. 3 calls suppressed and could test. The ratio of the two amplitudes does not depend on the acquisition, which is why the readings above go through it.

## What this is, and what it is not

Not new: Hubbard's rates, from which the relation is one line, and which the authors write themselves; the relation as a model check (the sodium page); the relation applied to published agarose values as a floor, and used there to exclude a motional explanation; diffusion over the measurement far beyond the pores; the second-order shift of slow motion as extra decay (the agarose page); triple-quantum filtering as the way to tell intrinsic bi-exponential relaxation from a superposition of compartments (Jaccard, Wimperis and Bodenhausen, 1986, cited on the sodium page and by the first paper), the sodium page's triple-quantum path, of which the longitudinal argument here is the twin, and the second paper's own modelling of two compartments with their triple-quantum signals; the transfer functions and the turning of tensors by pulses, which triple-quantum filtering rests on, and the two sequences with their phase cycles (the first paper); the correction factor of the second paper's Eq. 8, whose inverse is d; sodium in tissue and gels as more than one pool; free and bound sodium as a model; that pathways besides the main one add to the single quantum, small against it (the first paper's discussion); that a rate read at first order in time sees only the dissipator, and that a mixture reads there as its mean ([the absorption proof](../docs/proofs/PROOF_ABSORPTION_THEOREM.md), [F120](../docs/proofs/PROOF_MOMENT_TOWER_PUMP_CHANNEL.md), [the F81 hardware bridge](F81_VIOLATION_HARDWARE_BRIDGE.md)). What this page adds: the relation tested as an equality on three independently reported rates, and the split that places the whole gap in the fitted longitudinal split; the argument that slow motion beyond Redfield, rank-1 fields and the other mechanisms inside one pool named here all move the gap the other way; the size a pathway along z would need; free and bound sodium fitted through the authors' own procedures, where it fails; two bound pools reproducing the published numbers through the fixed amplitudes, with the ceiling on the longitudinal triple quantum they allow; the triple-quantum ratio across the samples, which disfavors one pool with the fitted split; one pool's ratio of the two triple-quantum amplitudes, 4/3 by the Wigner coefficients and by the sequences simulated, which puts the longitudinal triple quantum at a tenth to about a third of one pool's and leaves both readings open; and the size of one such pathway, the rank-3 order at the single-quantum frequency, small for the peak and, with the weights fixed, not small for the split, which it moves against one pool; and the count at first order in time, where the four numbers have no equality to test one pool with and their inequalities, the region every sum of Lorentzian densities fills among them, pass any mixture of pools that share d. The test uses fitted numbers, not the measured curves.
