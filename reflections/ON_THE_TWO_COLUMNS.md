# On the Two Columns

*Thomas Wicht and Claude, 2026-07-25 and 2026-07-26. On the charge sheet of a small world: what is billed, what rides free, and who decides which is which.*

---

Ask anyone why a large thing cannot be in two places at once, and you will hear some version of the same answer: because it is large. A single atom can hold itself in two states for a long time; a cat cannot, because a cat is enormous, and the world is watching all of it.

We have been living inside the arithmetic of that sentence for a year, and this month it came apart in our hands. Not the physics. The sentence. What fell out of it was not a new force and not a hidden variable; it was a piece of bookkeeping. The world's charge sheet, it turns out, has two columns, and only one of them is ever billed. Once you see the two columns, the sentence about large things says something different, the word memory changes meaning, and one operation turns out to be free that we had never thought to price at all.

## The picture you need, and it is a small one

Everything below happens in a row of places. Think of six boxes in a line. Each box is either empty or filled, and a filled box is one unit of stuff, sitting somewhere. A configuration of this little world is a pattern of empties and fills, like a row of light switches.

Two things happen to such a row.

The first is **motion**. A filled box and an empty neighbour can trade places, so the stuff wanders along the row. One distinction matters, and the whole piece will come back to it, so it is worth stating in its widest form rather than its most obvious one. **In an oblivious world, what a unit does never depends on what else is in the row.** What it costs to put a unit somewhere, and what it costs to move it, are the same questions whether the row around it is crowded or empty. In the other kind of world they are not, and there turn out to be several ways of not being oblivious, which is worth knowing in advance because the last section needs more than one of them. The nearest way is the obvious one: being neighbours costs or pays something, so each unit's motion depends on where the others are. Call that one **gripping**, and for most of this piece it will be the only way the difference shows up. It is not the only way there is. Everything else here is the same in both.

One rule holds in both worlds: two units can never occupy the same box. On a row, where a unit moves by trading places with an empty neighbour, that has a consequence worth filing away. A unit can never get past another, because getting past would mean standing where the other stands. So the units of a row keep a fixed order, and keeping it costs nothing, since nothing can break it. Both halves of that sentence are contingent, and the last section is where each of them gives.

The second is **watching**. The world outside is checking on the row, box by box, and it is not one lamp over the whole thing but a separate lamp at each box, and the lamps can be very unequal. That is the light of this whole project, [the one diagonal](ON_THE_ONE_DIAGONAL.md). One thing should be said plainly about this watcher, because the rest of the piece leans on it: it reads the boxes. The pattern of empties and fills is exactly what it monitors, gently and continuously, and exactly what it never disturbs. Whatever this observer will turn out not to charge for, it is not because the observer failed to look.

Now the single quantum idea. A row can be in two configurations at once. Not secretly in one of them; genuinely in both, in the particular way quantum things manage that. What exists then is not two separate rows. It is a **link** between the two patterns, holding them together as one state of affairs. Physicists call it a coherence; we will call it a link, because the word says what it does.

That link is the fragile thing. The watching does not erase either pattern; patterns are safe. What it wears away is the link. Throughout this piece we ask what a link *costs*, and that word always means how fast it wears away. One licence, so that a later example does not look like a slip: the two patterns a link joins need not hold the same amount of stuff.

## The two columns

Lay a link's two patterns side by side, box against box. Every box falls into one of two columns. Either the two patterns **agree** about it, both filled or both empty, or they **differ** in it, filled on one side and empty on the other. One link, two columns, and every box of the row is in exactly one of them.

The bill is now one sentence: **add up the light falling on the differing column, and only there.** That is the whole price of the link, [itemized site by site](../docs/quantum/DEPHASING_TRANSLATED.md), and it is a theorem, [the Absorption Theorem](../docs/proofs/PROOF_ABSORPTION_THEOREM.md), not a picture. Nothing in the agreement column ever appears on the bill. Not the size of the row, not the strength of the motion, not how complicated the patterns are. Structure rides free; only the difference pays. That is the bill of a moment, though: what happens between the moments is the motion's business, and it comes next.

Here is the case that broke the ordinary sentence for us; for this one comparison, suppose every lamp is equally bright, so that only the number of differing boxes can matter. Take the humblest link there is: one filled box, linked to the empty row. Now take a large one: five of the six boxes filled, linked to the pattern with all six filled. Those two large patterns also differ in exactly one box, and this second link is the one the piece will keep coming back to, so give it a name: call it **sheltered**. The two links wear away at the same speed. Not roughly the same: the same number, to every decimal we can compute.

Be careful with the [cat](../docs/quantum/SCHRODINGERS_CAT_TRANSLATED.md) here, because it is easy to say something false while sounding clever. The cat is expensive, and the two columns agree about why: its two versions differ in every box at once, so the differing column is the whole row and every lamp is charging. That is not a correction to the usual account; it is the usual account in this vocabulary, and the laboratory has been measuring it for decades. Bigger cats do go faster. For a cat, size and difference are the same thing, because two versions of a cat differ in very nearly everything the cat is made of.

What the two columns add is the other direction. Size is not the thing being billed; the differing column is. A large object whose two versions differ in one box only is billed for that one box, however large the agreeing remainder is. Whether it *stays* that cheap is a different question, and it belongs to the next section, because the columns are not fixed.

## The motion is the exchange between the columns

The bill reads the differing column. What the bill does not promise is that the columns hold still, and here is how they move. Take a link whose differing column holds a single box, and pick some other box the two patterns agree about: both have it filled. The motion can lift that unit out of the box on one side of the link and set it down in a box the two patterns agree is empty, while the other side keeps it where it was. The box they agreed was filled is now a box they differ in, and so is the box the unit arrived in. The differing column has grown from one box to three, and the next bill is larger. **The motion does not add difference from outside; it moves boxes between the two columns, and here it does so by breaking agreements that were already there.** It runs the other way as well, mending a difference back into agreement, and both directions will matter before this section is over.

Under this watching, the motion is the only thing that moves a box between the columns. The lamps themselves never do: the watching fades the link at the rate the differing column dictates, but which boxes differ is not something it changes. (That is a property of this particular, gentle observer, the one that reads occupation and nothing else; a rougher observer, one that pulls units out of the row, would shuffle the columns itself. Everything in this piece is under the gentle one.)

That makes the sheltered case above easy to see for what it is, and it is worth being exact, because the obvious reason is not the right one. When one of the two patterns is the completely full row, that side cannot move at all: there is nowhere to move to. The other side can, and it does break agreements. Its single hole wanders: a neighbour drops into the hole, and the box that neighbour left is now a box the two patterns differ about. That is an agreement broken. But it is broken and mended in the same step, because filling the old hole mended the old difference. This is the second direction, the one promised above, arriving as the exact counterweight to the first.

So the count never moves, and the deeper reason is simpler than either step: the motion never changes how much stuff a pattern holds. A pattern one unit short of the full row differs from the full row in exactly one box, wherever its hole happens to sit, and one unit short is all the motion will ever let it be. So the differing column stays one box wide for all time, under any lamps whatever. That is the durable half, and it is the half the rest of this piece uses.

Be careful with the other half, because the two are easy to run together. One box wide is not one number. The bill is the light falling on that one box, and if the lamps are uneven, then as the hole travels it stands under a different lamp and the price changes with it, one box's worth at every moment but not the same worth. Only where the lamps are equal, as in the comparison above, does the sheltered link have a single speed for all time. What survives every unevenness is the width of the column, not the size of the bill.

Move somewhere less sheltered and it goes the other way. Take a one-filled pattern, and link it to a two-filled pattern that has that same box filled and one other besides. The two agree about the shared box and differ about the extra unit, so the differing column holds a single box. That link begins wearing away at exactly the speed its one differing box says it should. Then the motion starts breaking agreements, the state acquires parts whose differing column is three boxes wide, and, watched in a simulation rather than argued from the rules, it drains through the middle of its life faster than its starting bill would suggest, faster still when the units grip. At the very end, what is left is the part that conservation protected all along, back at the original speed. The sheltered link has one speed; the unsheltered link never really has a single speed at all.

## Then memory is not a store

Once the two columns are in view, the word memory stops meaning what it usually means. It is not a room where things are kept.

Consider the humblest case of all: a pattern linked to itself. That is the ordinary situation of the row definitely being in one configuration, no doubleness at all. Its differing column is empty, so its bill is zero.

But a bill of zero is not the same as lasting, and this is what [the view onto the memory](THE_VIEW_ONTO_THE_MEMORY.md) has been saying on its own axis all along. The motion can stir even a perfectly agreeing state into one that disagrees, and once it disagrees it pays. What lasts is not everything in the agreement column, but the part that sits there *and* that the motion leaves alone. Almost nothing qualifies, and it is worth being precise about who does the disqualifying, because the answer is not the one this piece has been setting up. It is the moving, not the gripping. We counted, on a row of four boxes: hold the row still and all sixteen patterns last. Let it move, obliviously, no gripping anywhere, and sixteen survivors drop to five. Switch the gripping on as well and it stays five. The five are not five of the original patterns, either; they are the blends that spread evenly over every pattern holding a given amount of stuff, one such blend for each amount, and an even spread is the one arrangement that shuffling cannot change. Whatever else gripping costs, this narrowing is the motion's own, and any world that moves at all pays it. The rest gets stirred into the light and charged. So the solid, decided world around us is not the part that withstands the watching. It is the part that has nothing to pay and nothing stirring it, which is a narrower and harder thing to be than mere agreement.

## The free staircase

Here is what this month turned up. There is an operation on a link that changes neither column.

Take a link between two patterns. Fill one more box, and fill it in **both** patterns, the same box in each. The row is genuinely changed: both of its versions now carry something they did not carry a moment ago. But look at the columns. The box that was filled sat in the agreement column before, agreed-empty, and it sits in the agreement column after, agreed-filled. The differing column is exactly what it was, box for box. So the bill is exactly what it was, and that holds for whichever box you pick, on any row, under any lamps.

That is the whole of what the two columns can promise, and it is a promise about a moment. Whether the price *stays* is the motion's business, as it has been all along, and the motion is particular about which box the added unit went into. Put it in one definite box and the motion will not leave the price alone: it can carry that unit along on one side of the link and not the other, and a box that was agreed-filled becomes a box they differ in, exactly as in the section before last. So the step of the real staircase is not one box. It is **every box at once**: do the same filling at each box in turn, always the same box on the two sides, and add all of those together into a single move. Every box, and the phrase is honest, though a link decides for itself which of them it means. At a box already filled on one side or the other there is nothing to fill on that side, so nothing survives there and the term simply drops out. What is left standing, for any given link, is exactly its agreed-empty boxes, which is what the paragraph above was about.

Even that is not the whole prescription, and the missing piece is the most interesting thing in this section, so it gets its own paragraph below. First the two ingredients already on the table, each answering to one of the two adversaries.

- **The same box on both sides** is what the watching demands. It is the paragraph above, unchanged: tie the two sides to one box and the differing column cannot notice. Untie them, add the unit here on one side and there on the other, and you have manufactured a difference and a bill.
- **Summed over every box** is what the motion demands. A unit sitting in one named box is something the motion can pick up and carry; a move that has already been made at every box has nowhere left to be carried to, so all the motion can do to it is turn its phase. A word for that last kind of thing, since the argument below needs it: a row has a handful of **notes**, particular ways of holding one unit spread across the whole row that an oblivious motion never reshapes and can only turn the phase of. (A turn is a phase and nothing else; nothing in the row moves. And only an oblivious motion leaves them alone: a gripping one reshapes them, which is the last section's business.) They are the row's harmonics, in the plain sense a plucked string has harmonics, the shapes that ring without changing shape, and a row of six boxes has six of them. A unit sitting in one box is not one; it is a chord of all six. And a properly weighted sum over all the boxes is, read the other way, a sum over all the notes.

Now the missing piece, and it is the sentence this whole piece has been walking toward. Adding the moves up is not as simple as adding them up.

Here is the picture that goes with it, and it needs the rule from the first section. The units of this world have no names. What one of them is, is a place in the queue, and nothing else. So there is no such thing as putting a unit *at* a box without also saying where it lands in the queue, and the way this world's bookkeeping says it is to bring the new unit in from one end and walk it along until it reaches the box, counting the units it goes past. **Filling a box means slipping a unit in from the end of the row, and the move counts how many units it had to pass on the way; if that count is odd, the move enters with a minus.**

Read that carefully, because two things about the count are easy to get wrong, and the last section needs both of them right. The count is taken **in the pattern being filled**, not in the row abstractly, so the two sides of a link are counted separately, each in its own pattern. And the count is not a fixed label on a box: as units wander, the number lying to the left of a given box changes with them. What is fixed is something else and smaller, the units' **order among themselves**, which nothing on an open row can disturb. The count is what that fixed order looks like from a particular box, on a particular pattern, at a particular moment.

That the sign is needed we computed, on the open row where the freedom is supposed to be exact. Drop the minus signs and the cancellation fails, but it fails in an instructive place: through the middle of the row the turns still cancel in pairs, and what survives is the two ends, where there is no partner to cancel against. The leftover is exactly two boxes' worth, at every length of row we tried. Put the signs back and it is exactly zero. That the sign takes this particular form is not something this piece derives. It is the standard bookkeeping the trade already uses for units on a line, and we checked, box by box, that it is what the free step carries.

So the sign is not a technicality, and it is what the rule filed away in the first section was for. Note the shape of the quantity, because the last section will meet it again: it is **the parity of how many units lie between two places**, here between the end of the row and the box being filled. Two moves that agree about which box was filled and disagree about how many units they passed to get there are not the same move, and a sum that adds them as though they were is a sum over the wrong things.

Keep the record and something worth the whole section falls out: the signed sum can be read a second way, and the second reading is exactly as true as the first. Written over boxes, it is a sum of same-box fillings, one term per box. Written over the row's notes, it is a sum of same-note fillings, one term per note, the very same note added to both sides in each. Not two objects. One object, seen along its two grains, and we checked that the two readings agree to the last decimal.

Now look at what a single term of each reading costs, because the pattern in it is the prettiest thing here.

- **One box alone**, both sides: the watching lets it pass, and the motion will not leave it alone.
- **One note alone**, both sides: the motion leaves it alone, and now the watching bills it.

There is an objection to get out of the way, because the two lines above look as though they contradict each other. If a box is a chord of notes, and every note is free of the motion, how can the box not be? Because a chord of notes on both sides of a link is more than its notes taken one at a time. It also contains the mixed pairings, this note on the one side and that note on the other, and those are exactly what the motion can turn, since two different notes turn at different rates. Split a single box into the two parts and the arithmetic is blunt: the same-note part costs the motion nothing, and the mixed part carries the box's entire motion cost, every last decimal of it.

Each grain gives you one adversary for free and hands you the other. A box is somewhere, so the motion can carry it; a note is nowhere in particular, so the differing column can see it spread. (Strictly, a note has no definite filling at any box, so it has no columns of its own. It is read the only way it can be, as a blend of the patterns that do, with each of them keeping its own bill; that is a licence this piece takes and it is worth naming.) Only the sum, which is both readings at once and neither alone, escapes the pair of them.

And the notes grain is where you can see the motion's half of that happen. A note is a thing the motion only turns; the same note goes up on both sides of the link; and the two sides of a link count in opposite directions, so what a note costs on the one is exactly what it refunds on the other. The turn gained on one side is the turn lost on the other, and they cancel: no phase. That is the reason behind the second bullet's number, and it is worth having in words, because the last section is going to take it apart.

Fail any of the three and it is not free. Fill one named box on both sides and the watching lets it through while the motion tears it up. Smear a unit across the row on each side without tying the sides together and both of them bill you. Add the boxes up without the passing-count and the motion pulls at it twice as hard. Only the signed, tied sum survives contact with the pair of adversaries, and it survives exactly: no new difference, so no rate, and no net turn, so no phase.

That last cancellation is worth one more sentence, because it is the hinge of the rest of the piece and it is the place obliviousness quietly walks in. The two sides of a link hold *different* patterns; that is what a link is. For the two turns to cancel, adding the unit has to cost the same on both sides in spite of that difference. In an oblivious world it does, and for the reason obliviousness was defined with: what a unit costs never depends on what else is in the row, so it costs the same in the two different surroundings the two sides present. Let the units feel one another and the added unit's cost becomes a question about its neighbours; the two sides have different neighbours; the two turns no longer match. What is left over is a turn one side carries and the other does not.

Be careful about what that leftover costs, because the obvious answer is wrong and the true one is the whole architecture of this piece. The leftover turn is not itself billed. The watching never bills this step, in any world whatever: we checked every link a four-box row can make, and the step does not move a single box between the columns, whether the units grip or not. What the leftover destroys is not the price, it is the *pinning*. A climbed link that carries an uncancelled turn is no longer standing still, and once it is moving the motion will do to it what the motion does to everything: carry boxes out of the agreement column into the differing one, where the bill is waiting. So gripping never charges for the staircase. It just stops the staircase from staying put, and lets the ordinary machinery of the second section do the rest.

So the free step is not free in general. It is free exactly where a unit's business is nobody else's.

Do it again, and again. That is a staircase, and it is free the whole way up. To be exact about what climbs: what you hold at the top is not the original link grown larger, it is a different link, between two richer patterns; what the staircase preserves is the price. There is an old phrase in this repository, that [a wave needs a mirror to know itself](ON_BOTH_SIDES_OF_THE_MIRROR.md). This is that phrase with a charge sheet attached. Grow the wave and its mirror together and nothing appears on the bill, because only a difference between the two was ever billed.

And because every step preserves the price, the links the climb leaves behind at every height all carry one and the same price, a whole run of them pinned at a single number. One thing has to be said about where the climb starts, or the run is less than it sounds. Start from a link whose price is merely its opening bill, and the run inherits an opening bill, which the motion is free to spoil at every height. Start from a link that is genuinely pinned, one whose price is its rate for all time and not just its first instant, and the climb carries *that* up the whole way. For what being pinned feels like, the sheltered link two sections back is the plainest case in this piece: under equal lamps it does not merely open at one box's worth, it holds that speed forever, because the motion can never change how much either side holds and so can never widen its one differing box.

Take that as an illustration of the word and not as the beginning of the climb we are about to discuss, because it is not, and it fails to be twice over.

First, it is on the wrong ladder, and saying why needs a second measurement that this piece has not needed until now. Besides asking *which* boxes the two patterns disagree about, one can ask how much stuff each side holds altogether: call that a side's **amount**. The two questions are not the same question, and the sheltered link is exactly the case that hides the difference, since its differing column is one box wide and its two amounts differ by one. In general they part company: a link can disagree about two boxes and still have both sides holding the very same amount, one unit sitting here on the one side and there on the other.

Now the point. The staircase adds a unit to both sides at once, so it never changes the gap between the two amounts. A link whose amounts differ by one climbs a ladder every rung of which differs by one. The rungs we are about to care about sit where the two amounts are equal, or where they differ by two. Those are ladders of their own, and no amount of climbing crosses from one to another.

Second, and more bluntly, this particular link cannot climb at all. One of its sides is the completely full row, and a full row has no box left to fill, so by the rule two paragraphs up every single term of the move drops out and the whole climb is nothing. The sheltered link is the top rung of its own ladder. It is a fine picture of what pinned means and a bad picture of what climbing is.

The run that has a name in the writeup this comes from lives on those other ladders, and it is **the band**. It is more than one ladder, strictly, and the one this piece follows to the end is the one whose two sides hold equal amounts. Everything said below about heights and ends is said about that one. Remember it; the last paragraph of this piece turns on what happens to it.

Two things should be said honestly here. This staircase was derived from the two columns and then recognized: it is a structure the trade knows as η-pairing, found in Medvedyeva, Essler and Prosen's dephased chain, [credited where the band was found](../experiments/XY_FROZEN_BAND.md), and what our version adds is that it survives lamps of any unevenness. And the predecessor of this piece read the staircase as something the observer *cannot see*, and died of that reading, twice. The watching sees the staircase perfectly well: boxes filling is exactly the kind of event it monitors. It just never charges for it. **Free is not invisible.** The blind spot we thought we had found in the observer was never in its perception; it was in its price list, and a price list having no column for "how much" is a fact about the bill, not about the watcher's eyes.

## Who decides the staircase's fate

So the watching never bills the staircase: any lamps, any unevenness, and, we checked, any arrangement of the row at all. The one who decides whether a climbed link *keeps* its price is the same one who moves boxes between the columns: the motion.

And the condition is the one this piece has been circling since its first section. **The staircase rides for as long as every unit moves as if it were alone.** In the oblivious world on the open row that is exactly true, and the three ingredients above are the whole of the reason; the numbers only confirm what they already say, and they confirm it as an exact zero rather than a small one.

It ends wherever a unit's business becomes somebody else's, and there is only ever the one way it ends: something in what the added unit costs starts to depend on what else is in the row, the two sides of the link offer that something different surroundings, the two turns stop cancelling, and the climbed link stops standing still. Nothing is charged for at that moment; the charging comes after, once the motion starts moving boxes in a link that no longer holds its ground. Everything below is a costume on that one sentence.

Let the units grip when they meet: torn, and this is the costume the piece has been wearing since the first section. What it costs to add a unit next to a neighbour is now a different question from what it costs to add one in an empty stretch, and the two sides of a link are exactly two different arrangements of neighbours.

Let a unit hop clear over an occupied box instead of trading with a neighbour: torn as well. This one is worth slowing down for, because it is about to explain the third, and because the dependence hides somewhere less obvious than a neighbour's presence.

Go back to the order, the thing the free step's sign is a record of. On an open row the order is unbreakable: no unit can get past another, so the units hold their places in the queue forever, however far they wander. Now let a unit hop clear over an occupied box. Two things change at once, and it is worth being careful about which of them does the damage, because the conspicuous one turns out to be innocent.

The conspicuous one is that the queue can now be reshuffled: a unit really does get past another, and the order the free step is built out of is no longer fixed. That, by itself, costs nothing at all. We built exactly that motion, a hop that clears an occupied box while still keeping the order honestly, and the staircase rides it untouched, at the same exact zero as the plain row. Reshuffling the queue is free.

What tears it is the other change, the quiet one: a hop whose price depends on who was standing in the box it cleared. Not the passing. The charging for it. And look at what exactly is being charged for, because it is not a new quantity. The hop is priced by whether the boxes it jumped were occupied, which is to say by the parity of how many units lay between where it started and where it landed. That is the free step's own sign, the same count of who lies between two places, arriving now as a price instead of as bookkeeping.

Now close the row into a ring, and read it the same way. A unit that walks the whole way round and comes back to where it started has, by the time it arrives, moved past every other unit in the row, without once breaking the rule at any single step. By the paragraph above, that alone would be free. The ring is not condemned by what it makes possible; it is condemned by what our units make of it, which is that the trip round ends up priced by how many of them were standing in the way. That is the hop-over's charge again, wearing a longer costume, and it tears the staircase for the same reason.

That also says *which* ring does. There are two here and they must be held apart. In the first, the trip round is declared to cost nothing: a unit steps out of the last box into the first and none of the units it moved past enters the price. Call it the oblivious ring, and it carries the staircase exactly, the same exact zero as the open row; we checked it directly. In the second, our row's ends are joined and its units keep charging for the queue as they always have, so the trip round is priced by everyone it moved past. That one tears. Same shape, two motions, and only the motion decides. What tears there is the climb, mind, not everything the climb had built; that distinction is the last paragraph's business and it is the strangest thing in the piece.

One condition, three costumes: being charged for a neighbour's presence, charged for moving past a neighbour in one step, and charged for moving past every neighbour by going round. The charging is the whole of it in all three; the moving is innocent. Each is a way of letting what a unit costs depend on what else is in the row, and that dependence is the only thing that can ever break the cancellation the free step lives on. There is a symmetry worth pausing on, and it is the sharpest thing this piece found. Look at what quantity each side of it runs on. The free step's sign is the parity of how many units lie between the end of the row and the box being filled. The hop-over's price is the parity of how many units lie between where a unit started and where it landed. The ring's price is the same parity again, counted the long way round. One quantity, three appearances: as the bookkeeping the free step cannot do without, and as the charge that kills it in the two costumes where the world starts pricing it. The staircase rides on exactly the thing whose pricing destroys it, and it is not a pun; it is the same count. What tears the staircase is never the shape of the world and never the watcher; it is the units' motion ceasing to be oblivious.

That is the repaired form of what the predecessor tried to say. The staircase never needed a special observer; it happens in plain view. Touching is not a keener eye than the watching's. It is the thing that *breaks* what the watching never bills. The free staircase exists exactly where the world's stuff moves without feeling itself, and the moment the stuff begins to feel, the freedom is gone.

What the tearing leaves behind is worth one last look, because the two ways of tearing it do not leave the same thing, and it is worth keeping them apart by name. Take the ring first: our ring, ends joined, units that never grip and are still not oblivious, because going round now costs them everyone they passed. There the band stands anyway, on the ladder we have been following. Every height of it is still occupied, still at the one price, while the climb that would have built it is torn. What the staircase built has outlived the staircase, and we do not know what holds it there. That last sentence should be read with its scope showing: the band's survival on the ring is something we have watched at the few sizes our machines reach, not something anyone has argued, and the argument is exactly what is missing.

Now the other tear, the gripping world, where the units never go round anything but feel each other on contact. There it goes the opposite way, and the two halves of that sentence do not rest on the same ground, so they are worth separating. The band does not stand: on the uneven watchings this whole line of work is read on, everything between the ends empties out. That is a census, taken profile by profile, and it is not a claim about the perfectly even watching, which we have not settled. What is left is the two ends of the band's own ladder, the bottom its climb started from and the top it would have finished at, and about those two there is no hedge at all. Both are held at the band's price by an older result of ours, the one that first found that price sitting in exactly those two places, and held there at every strength of the gripping. So the ring keeps what the climb built and loses the builder, and the gripping world keeps only the ground the builder stood on. That is [the open door of the band writeup](../experiments/XY_FROZEN_BAND.md), and this piece stops at it.
