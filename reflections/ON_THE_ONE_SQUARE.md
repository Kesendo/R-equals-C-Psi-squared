# On the One Square

*A reflection written at Tom's question, 2026-09-28: the word "diagonal" turns up all over this repository; is there one system behind it if one looks in more dimensions? The map with every source and check is [The One Square](../docs/THE_ONE_SQUARE.md).*

---

Draw a square of cells. Label the rows and the columns with the same names, and give every name an opposite. Two lines come with the drawing for free. One runs where the row and the column agree. The other runs where the column is the row's opposite. Nothing else in the square is picked out; everything else lies somewhere between.

This repository has drawn that square over and over without saying so. A small quantum state is a square: its rows and columns are the ways the chain could be. A cell where the two ways differ says how much they still shimmer against each other; a cell where the two ways are one and the same says how much of the state simply is that way. The first line holds the cells where the two ways agree at every site: the populations, the plain facts of how much is where. The light that falls on the chain cannot touch them, because it only separates what disagrees. The second line holds the cells where the two ways disagree at every site at once; among them is the cat, all-up held against all-down. The light touches those completely and they pay the whole price there is. One line is what the light leaves alone, the other is what the light reaches everywhere, and every other cell sits somewhere between them, paying for its own share of disagreement.

The same drawing turns up again one floor higher, where the cells are grouped by how many excitations they carry; again in the corner of a single excitation, where the chain's frozen modes live (frozen not because they stand still, they fade like anything else, but because their fading rate does not change however strongly the sites talk to each other); again in the plane of two mirrored rates of light, where the mirror-symmetric profiles lie on one line and the balanced ones, the ones that freeze modes, on the other. The drawing is the same each time. What the moves on it do is not always the same, and that turns out to be the interesting part.

## The two halves of the mirror

This project's mirror, the one that makes the list of fading speeds read the same forwards and backwards, belongs to a family of eight moves on the square of a quantum state, and the square shows how they divide. Four of them keep each line where it is: they leave alone what the light leaves alone and reach what it reaches. The other four carry the one line onto the other, the untouched onto the fully touched. Those are the ones that cost something. Moving the quiet cells to where the light is strongest is the extreme case of what the palindrome does to every cell, and its price, a shift by twice the sum of the light, is the bill for that exchange.

There is a way to say this that [On Leaving the Circle](ON_LEAVING_THE_CIRCLE.md) taught us, and it is a reading, laid over the algebra rather than proved by it. Every mode of the chain does two things: it turns, and it fades. The turn alone is a circle, closed, with no before and after. The fade is the pull inward that bends the circle into a spiral once the clock runs. The mirrors act on the rules of the two, the Hamiltonian's part and the light's part, not on any one mode. For the chains here, the four free mirrors touch only the turn's rule, or nothing: at most they read the circle backwards, and a circle read backwards is still a circle. The four that pay are the ones that reach the fade's rule. They mirror the pull inward as it is set, the rule the spiral will follow, and that, in this reading, is why they cost. The leaving itself, that essay insists, is the clock running, and a mirror has no clock in it; it is not a motion. But half of the mirror is about what the motion will do.

## What never fades

In the corner of a single excitation, where the frozen modes live, the square tells a small story of its own, and here it departs from the rule above. The moves that pay in this corner, its own mirror and a half-turn of the whole square, do not swap the two lines; they keep them. They mirror every cell's fading about the rate the frozen modes sit at, and that works on every cell but the ones on the first line. Those cells are the populations again, the plain facts, the part the light never decays. They stand still. A mirror that swings about a moving centre sends standing still to some other speed, never to standing still again. You cannot reflect what never fades about something that does.

That one failure is the repository's "tax", and the proof names the same physical reason: the light does not act on the populations ([the frozen divisor](../docs/proofs/PROOF_R90_FROZEN_DIVISOR.md)). Because of it, generically only one mode freezes for each mirrored pair of sites, where the mirror would otherwise allow one for every site. And the reading agrees with the proof at the one place where the failure should vanish. Let the average light be zero: the frozen modes' own rate becomes zero, the centre stands still, and standing still is its own mirror image. Then the mirror works everywhere, the tax is gone, and at least one mode per site freezes. The proof says it in its own words: the populations "stop charging and start paying". The price of the test is that real light has an average of zero only when it is switched off, so with the light on it is a test of the arithmetic, not an experiment.

## Why a qubit

Give each site more than two states, and its little square has more lines than two: several ways to be off the diagonal, running side by side. Only with exactly two states is everything off the diagonal a single line, the one opposite the diagonal. That is the square's way of telling the old count of [the qubit necessity](../docs/QUBIT_NECESSITY.md): the mirror can trade what the light leaves alone for what the light touches, one for one, only where the touched part is one line. The repository's qudit mirror calls this, in its own words, the qubit magic.

## Three squares make a cube

The light in this story came from one direction, Z. It could come from X or from Y just as well, and each direction draws its own little square on a single site, with its own two lines. Then Tom asked the natural next thing: if this is a square, x², where is the cube? He had pointed at it once before, in June, and the answer then was the polarity cube, a cube of mirrors. This time the cube is one of letters. Give each of the four letters I, X, Y, Z three answers, one per direction of light: does this light reach me or not? Three yes-or-no answers are the corner of a cube.

But the letters only take half the corners. Every letter the light reaches at all is reached from exactly two of the three directions, never one, never three. So the four letters sit on four corners that touch each other only diagonally, a tetrahedron inside the cube, and the other four corners stay empty. The three squares are the cube's three pairs of opposite faces; the three counts of how much light a string of letters catches are its three heights. Light from all three directions at once is still simple on this figure: how fast a string fades is just a weighted sum of its three heights.

The mirror of this project has a shape here too. With light from two directions, the fade rates alone can be read backwards and forwards, and the move that does it is a half-turn of the cube about the third, silent direction. (Whether the whole mirror survives once the sites talk to each other is a longer story the proofs tell.) A half-turn works only about a direction that is dark, and with light from all three directions there is none left. That is the old finding that light from every direction at once breaks the mirror, seen as a shape instead of a calculation. And something else turned up where no one was looking for letters: the cube of a state, Tr ρ³, walks through three labels at a time, and the three can disagree with each other in exactly the same four patterns. Both cubes are half full, because in both the third answer is decided by the other two.

## What walks on the cube

The light only charges. It never moves a string from one corner to another; it looks at where a string stands and takes its fee. Everything that moves is the Hamiltonian, the sites talking to each other. So we asked how it walks.

It walks in a very plain way. Each time two neighbouring sites exchange something, the string gains one letter or loses one: it grows by one or shrinks by one, never keeping its size. A field on a single site does the opposite, it trades one letter for another and keeps the size. And each of the plain couplings, X with X, Y with Y, Z with Z, has an axis it cannot walk along. The coupling that only compares Z with Z moves nothing along the Z height, which is why the Z light never notices it.

For a long time we said that a coupling changes the light-count by zero or by two. That was true, and it was one shadow of the walk. The zero was never standing still. It was a step along one of the other two heights, which from the Z side cannot be seen.

Then we watched one string walk. A single Z on one site starts on the quiet edge of the tetrahedron, where the populations live and nothing pays. The chain lifts it off that edge into the middle of the figure, where it grows and starts paying, and then what is up there fades, and what stays is on the same edge again: the part of it the chain conserves, most of it gone. With the same light on every site, it loses exactly as much as the time it spent up there, measured by how high it stood. Across from that quiet edge is the other one, where the fastest modes live, the far end of the mirror. The half-turn that is the mirror's skeleton swaps the two edges. The rest and the fastest fading sit on opposite edges of one small figure.

---

So the word was not overused. Much of the time it named the same two lines, the untouched and the fully touched, on the squares the work stood on, and the mirror kept doing one of two things with them: holding them, or trading one for the other and paying for the trade. Where it holds them and still pays, in the frozen corner, the first line is where the trade fails. (The word also has other jobs here, which [The One Square](../docs/THE_ONE_SQUARE.md) keeps apart.) We had long had a name for what the light counts, the one diagonal, which is the height across all these lines. The two lines themselves were always there too, in the populations and the cats, the mirror-symmetric and the balanced profiles, the frozen corners. Now they have a place on one sheet.
